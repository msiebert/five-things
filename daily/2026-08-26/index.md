  - id: "2026-08-26-1"
    question: "What Nantucket whaling ship, rammed and sunk by an enraged sperm whale in 1820 and leaving its crew adrift for months in an ordeal that included cannibalism, directly inspired the climax of Moby-Dick?"
    answer: "Essex"
    topic: arts
    explanation: >-
      In November 1820, the whaleship Essex was struck twice and sunk by a
      large sperm whale in the South Pacific, an almost unheard-of act of
      apparent deliberate aggression from an animal whalers usually hunted
      without fear of retaliation. The twenty crew members fled in small
      whaleboats and endured a three-month ordeal of starvation that drove
      some to cannibalism before the few survivors were rescued. Herman
      Melville met the son of the Essex's captain and read a survivor's
      first-hand account while researching his novel, and the ship's fate
      became the direct model for the Pequod's destruction.
  - id: "2026-08-26-2"
    question: "What real albino sperm whale, notorious among 19th-century Pacific whalers and profiled in an 1839 magazine article near a Chilean island, is thought to have lent Melville the idea of a giant white whale?"
    answer: "Mocha Dick"
    topic: arts
    explanation: >-
      Decades before Melville's novel, whalers in the Pacific told stories
      of a huge, pale-skinned sperm whale that had survived and won dozens
      of encounters with hunting ships near Chile's Mocha Island. Writer
      Jeremiah Reynolds turned those stories into a widely read 1839
      magazine account describing the whale as "white as wool," giving
      Melville a real-world template for an unusually large, aggressive,
      and recognizable whale years before he combined that image with the
      Essex disaster to create his fictional antagonist.
  - id: "2026-08-26-3"
    question: "What crucial closing section of Moby-Dick did Melville's British publisher, releasing the book as 'The Whale,' accidentally omit — leaving British reviewers baffled about how a narrator who seemingly died with the ship could have told the story?"
    answer: "Epilogue"
    topic: arts
    explanation: >-
      The novel's first-person narrator appears to go down with the ship
      in its final chapter, and only a short closing passage reveals how
      he alone survived to tell the story. Melville's British publisher,
      Richard Bentley, heavily edited the text for its October 1851
      release and dropped this passage entirely, apparently by accident
      during the cuts. Without it, British critics singled out what looked
      like a glaring structural error — a dead man narrating his own
      story — and used it as evidence the book was carelessly written,
      unaware the American edition published weeks later resolved it.
  - id: "2026-08-26-4"
    question: "What name is given to the surge of critical and popular interest in Melville's work during the 1920s, decades after his 1891 death, that rescued Moby-Dick from near-obscurity?"
    answer: "Melville Revival"
    topic: arts
    explanation: >-
      Herman Melville died in 1891 largely forgotten as a novelist, working
      his final years as a customs inspector in New York with Moby-Dick
      long out of print and unsold. Interest in his work reawakened around
      the centennial of his birth in 1919, as critics and scholars began
      reassessing his novels' psychological depth and formal ambition,
      a reappraisal now known as the Melville Revival. That renewed
      attention through the 1920s is largely why Moby-Dick is read today
      as a landmark of American literature rather than a forgotten
      commercial failure.
  - id: "2026-08-26-5"
    question: "About how many copies made up the first American printing of Moby-Dick in 1851 — a run so small that copies still hadn't sold out by the time Melville died four decades later?"
    answer: "2,915"
    topic: arts
    explanation: >-
      Harper & Brothers printed just 2,915 copies for the book's American
      debut in November 1851. Sales started reasonably well, with around
      1,500 copies sold in the first eleven days, but then slowed sharply,
      and the book never came close to earning out. Melville made only a
      few hundred dollars from American sales over his lifetime, and part
      of that first print run, including unbound sheets, was later
      destroyed in a warehouse fire — the novel remained a commercial
      disappointment until its rediscovery decades after his death.
