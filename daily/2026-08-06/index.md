  - id: "2026-08-06-1"
    question: "How many languages of spoken greetings does the Golden Record aboard Voyager 1 carry into interstellar space?"
    answer: "55"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      The Golden Record, a gold-plated copper phonograph disc bolted to
      Voyager 1's exterior, carries spoken greetings in 55 languages, from
      Akkadian — spoken some 6,000 years ago — to modern Mandarin, chosen by
      a committee led by astronomer Carl Sagan. Alongside the greetings it
      holds 115 images, natural and human sounds like whale calls and
      thunder, and 90 minutes of music spanning Bach to Chuck Berry. Its
      gold plating and the vacuum of space should keep it playable for
      over a billion years, though Voyager 1 isn't headed toward any
      particular star, so it's unlikely to be found for tens of thousands
      of years, if ever.
  - id: "2026-08-06-2"
    question: "About how many watts of electrical output do Voyager 1's plutonium-powered generators lose every year as their fuel decays?"
    answer: "Four"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      Voyager 1 is powered by three radioisotope thermoelectric generators
      that convert heat from decaying plutonium-238 into electricity. They
      produced about 470 watts at launch in 1977, but lose roughly four
      watts of output every year as the plutonium's heat output wanes and
      the thermocouples degrade. Nearly 49 years on, that leaves the
      spacecraft running on less than half its original power, forcing
      NASA engineers to permanently power down individual science
      instruments one at a time to keep the mission going.
  - id: "2026-08-06-3"
    question: "What science instrument did NASA permanently power down on Voyager 1 in April 2026 after a February roll maneuver nearly tripped its undervoltage safety fault?"
    answer: "LECP"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      The Low-Energy Charged Particles (LECP) instrument had measured ions,
      electrons, and cosmic rays around Voyager 1 almost continuously since
      1977. In February 2026, a routine roll maneuver caused an unexpected
      dip in the spacecraft's power that came close to triggering its
      automatic "undervoltage" fault protection — a self-preserving
      shutdown that would have forced a risky, multi-day recovery. To avoid
      that risk, JPL engineers commanded LECP off in April 2026, freeing up
      power to keep Voyager 1's remaining instruments and its radio
      transmitter running.
  - id: "2026-08-06-4"
    question: "As of 2026, about how many hours does a one-way radio signal take to cross the roughly 15 billion miles between Earth and Voyager 1?"
    answer: "23"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      Voyager 1 is now more than 15 billion miles (24 billion kilometers)
      from Earth, so its radio signals — traveling at the speed of light —
      take around 23 hours to make the one-way trip. That means engineers
      wait nearly two full days just to confirm a command reached the
      spacecraft and see its response come back, turning even routine
      maintenance into a days-long exercise in patience.
  - id: "2026-08-06-5"
    question: "About how many kilobytes of memory does Voyager 1's entire onboard computer system have — less than a single photo on a modern phone?"
    answer: "70"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      Voyager 1 flies with three redundant computer systems built in the
      1970s, whose combined memory adds up to roughly 70 kilobytes — far
      less than the several megabytes a single photo takes on a modern
      smartphone. Data is recorded on an eight-track digital tape recorder
      before being radioed home, since the spacecraft launched years before
      hard drives were standard equipment. Despite running 1970s-era
      software, JPL engineers have kept it going for decades by remotely
      patching and reprogramming the systems as components fail.
