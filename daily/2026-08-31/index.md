  - id: "2026-08-31-1"
    question: "What term describes ground -- soil, rock, or sediment -- that has stayed at or below the freezing point of water for at least two consecutive years, no matter how much snow or ice sits on top of it?"
    answer: "Permafrost"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      Permafrost is defined purely by temperature, not by how icy or how
      snow-covered it looks on the surface, and it can extend from just
      below the ground down thousands of feet in the coldest regions. It
      underlies roughly a quarter of the land in the Northern Hemisphere,
      across Siberia, Alaska, northern Canada, and high mountain ranges,
      and much of it has stayed frozen continuously since the last Ice Age.
      Because organic matter trapped inside it barely decomposes while
      frozen, permafrost acts as a vast cold-storage vault for ancient
      carbon, plant material, and even long-dead animals, which is exactly
      why its thawing matters so much for climate and human health.
  - id: "2026-08-31-2"
    question: "What name do scientists give to the ice-rich, Ice Age-era type of permafrost found across Siberia and Alaska that packs in roughly a quarter of all the organic carbon locked in frozen ground worldwide, despite covering only a small share of the permafrost region?"
    answer: "Yedoma"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      Yedoma is a distinctive kind of permafrost made of windblown silt
      riddled with massive wedges of ground ice, built up over tens of
      thousands of years during the Pleistocene. Because it's so ice-rich,
      it's also unusually unstable once it thaws: rather than gradually
      settling, yedoma ground can collapse abruptly into slumping craters
      and lakes, a process called thermokarst. That sudden collapse exposes
      long-buried organic matter to rapid microbial decay, releasing both
      carbon dioxide and methane far faster than steadier, drier types of
      permafrost do.
  - id: "2026-08-31-3"
    question: "What genus of giant, amoeba-infecting virus did French scientists revive alive from a 48,500-year-old chunk of Siberian permafrost in 2023, the discovery behind headlines about 'zombie viruses' thawing out of the Arctic?"
    answer: "Pandoravirus"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      Researchers led by microbiologist Jean-Michel Claverie pulled more
      than a dozen previously unknown viruses out of ancient Siberian
      permafrost cores, all still capable of infecting amoebae after tens
      of thousands of years frozen. The oldest, a new pandoravirus strain
      found in yedoma-layer permafrost, was dated to roughly 48,500 years
      old, making it one of the most ancient viruses ever revived. The
      viruses studied only target amoebae, not humans, but the discovery
      raised a genuinely open question researchers are still investigating:
      whether older, more dangerous pathogens could survive the same deep
      freeze and reawaken as permafrost keeps thawing.
  - id: "2026-08-31-4"
    question: "A 2018 USGS study found frozen Arctic permafrost holds about twice as much of what toxic, silvery heavy metal as all the world's oceans, atmosphere, and other soils combined?"
    answer: "Mercury"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      USGS hydrologist Paul Schuster and colleagues estimated that northern
      permafrost holds around 1,656 gigagrams of natural mercury, roughly
      32 million gallons' worth, making frozen ground the single largest
      mercury reservoir on the planet. That's about twice as much as is
      currently held in the ocean, atmosphere, and all other soils
      combined, and roughly ten times more than humanity has emitted from
      burning fossil fuels over the past three decades. As permafrost
      thaws, some of that mercury can wash into rivers and enter the food
      chain, raising health concerns for fish, wildlife, and communities
      that rely on them for food.
  - id: "2026-08-31-5"
    question: "What nickname do observers give to boreal forests, common across permafrost regions of Alaska and Siberia, where the trees end up leaning every which way once the frozen ground under their roots thaws unevenly and subsides?"
    answer: "Drunken forest"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      When permafrost thaws unevenly, patches of ground sink at different
      rates while others stay firm, tilting the trees rooted in it into a
      chaotic tangle of angles instead of the tidy vertical stands typical
      of healthy boreal forest. The effect is common enough across the
      Arctic and subarctic that it has become one of the most visible,
      photographed signs of permafrost degradation, showing up in Alaska,
      Siberia, and northern Canada wherever the ground beneath tree roots
      is losing its structural footing. Scientists use the spread of
      drunken forests as a rough visual proxy for tracking how fast
      permafrost thaw is spreading across a landscape.
