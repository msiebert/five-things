  - id: "2026-08-14-1"
    question: "What 2,000-year-old bronze device, recovered from a Greek shipwreck off Antikythera in 1901, is now recognized as the world's oldest known analog computer?"
    answer: "Antikythera mechanism"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      In 1901, sponge divers exploring a Roman-era shipwreck off the Greek
      island of Antikythera pulled up a corroded lump of bronze that turned
      out to be a geared mechanical device, built around 150-100 BCE. Turning
      a hand crank drove dozens of interlocking bronze gears that displayed
      the positions of the Sun, Moon, and five visible planets, tracked
      calendars, and predicted eclipses. It's the most sophisticated known
      mechanism to survive from antiquity, and nothing of comparable
      engineering complexity is known to have been built again for well over
      a thousand years.
  - id: "2026-08-14-2"
    question: "What ancient eclipse-prediction cycle of 223 lunar months was mechanically encoded in the Antikythera mechanism's spiral back dial?"
    answer: "Saros"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      The mechanism's lower back dial is a spiral divided into 223 months,
      tracing out the Saros cycle, a pattern the Babylonians had discovered
      centuries earlier by which the Sun, Moon, and Earth return to nearly
      the same relative positions roughly every 18 years. By reading a
      pointer's position along that spiral, an operator could find out when
      the next solar or lunar eclipse was due, and inscriptions on the dial
      even flagged the expected type and timing of each one.
  - id: "2026-08-14-3"
    question: "What four-year Panhellenic athletic cycle, including the games at Olympia, did one of the Antikythera mechanism's small front dials track?"
    answer: "Olympiad"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      Tucked among the astronomical displays is a small subsidiary dial that
      has nothing to do with the sky at all: it counted off the four-year
      cycle of Panhellenic games, including the Olympics at Olympia, along
      with other regional athletic festivals. Researchers only confirmed
      this function in 2008 after re-reading faint inscriptions on the dial
      with reflectance imaging, showing the device doubled as a civic
      calendar for the games that structured Greek public life.
  - id: "2026-08-14-4"
    question: "How many surviving bronze gears did modern X-ray and CT imaging reveal packed inside the corroded Antikythera mechanism?"
    answer: "30"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      For most of the 20th century, the mechanism's true complexity was
      hidden inside heavily corroded, fused lumps of bronze. A 2005 CT
      scanning campaign by the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project
      finally let researchers see through the corrosion, revealing at least
      30 surviving interlocking gears, some with teeth just over a
      millimeter apart, and researchers believe the complete original
      mechanism held even more.
  - id: "2026-08-14-5"
    question: "Whose ancient theory of the Moon's uneven orbital speed did the Antikythera mechanism model more accurately than any written astronomy for centuries after, using a clever pin-and-slot gear?"
    answer: "Hipparchus"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      The Moon appears to speed up and slow down slightly as it crosses the
      sky, an effect now explained by its elliptical orbit but which Greek
      astronomers like Hipparchus modeled geometrically. The mechanism's
      designers reproduced that irregular motion mechanically with a
      pin-and-slot gear pair, one gear riding slightly off-center of the
      other, that sped up and slowed down exactly like the real Moon. It's
      considered one of the earliest known applications of an epicyclic
      gear train, and it modeled lunar motion more accurately than the best
      surviving written astronomy for centuries afterward.
