  - id: "2026-09-03-1"
    question: "What small Buddhist kingdom in the eastern Himalayas, wedged between India and China, measures its national progress with a 'Gross National Happiness' index instead of GDP?"
    answer: "Bhutan"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Bhutan is a landlocked country of well under a million people,
      tucked in the eastern Himalayas between India and China. It was an
      absolute monarchy until 2008, when it peacefully transitioned to a
      constitutional democracy at the reigning king's own initiative
      rather than under outside pressure. Bhutan is best known for its
      unusual approach to development, its deep Buddhist traditions, and
      a historic wariness of outside influence — from capping how many
      tourists can visit to, until recently, banning television outright.
      The facts below cover just how far that streak of national
      idiosyncrasy runs.
  - id: "2026-09-03-2"
    question: "What 7,570-meter peak in Bhutan, whose name translates to 'White Peak of the Three Spiritual Brothers,' holds the title of the world's tallest mountain never to have been climbed?"
    answer: "Gangkhar Puensum"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Bhutan banned mountaineering above 6,000 meters in 1994 out of
      respect for the deities believed to inhabit its highest summits,
      then banned all mountaineering outright in 2003. Gangkhar Puensum,
      straddling the border with Tibet, has never had a confirmed ascent
      — a few expeditions reached nearby subsidiary peaks in the 1980s
      before the ban took effect, but the true summit remains untouched.
      Local Buddhist and pre-Buddhist tradition holds that the mountain
      is home to protective spirits, making a climb not just illegal but,
      to many Bhutanese, spiritually inappropriate.
  - id: "2026-09-03-3"
    question: "What Bhutanese king coined the term 'Gross National Happiness' in 1972, declaring it more important than Gross Domestic Product as a measure of his country's progress?"
    answer: "Jigme Singye Wangchuck"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Jigme Singye Wangchuck became Bhutan's fourth king at just 16 years
      old after his father's sudden death, and used the phrase almost
      offhandedly in a 1972 interview to explain why he wasn't chasing
      GDP growth for its own sake. The idea eventually grew into a formal
      policy framework built on four pillars — sustainable development,
      environmental conservation, cultural preservation, and good
      governance — later expanded into a nine-domain index that Bhutan's
      government still uses to screen major policy decisions today.
  - id: "2026-09-03-4"
    question: "Bhutan's constitution permanently requires the country to maintain forest cover of at least what percentage of its total land area?"
    answer: "60%"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Adopted in 2008, Bhutan's constitution was the first in the world
      to enshrine a specific, permanent environmental target this way —
      forest cover can never legally fall below 60%. Bhutan comfortably
      clears that floor today, with roughly 70% of the country under
      forest, which combined with its small population and hydropower-
      heavy energy grid makes it one of the only carbon-negative
      countries on Earth, absorbing more carbon dioxide than it emits.
  - id: "2026-09-03-5"
    question: "In what year did Bhutan become the last country in the world to legalize television and the internet?"
    answer: "1999"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Television had been officially outlawed since 1989 as a threat to
      Bhutan's cultural heritage, until the state broadcaster Bhutan
      Broadcasting Service launched its first transmission on June 2,
      1999, timed to coincide with the king's silver jubilee. Internet
      access arrived simultaneously, and within a few years dozens of
      cable channels flooded in — a shift so abrupt that some Bhutanese
      officials later pointed to it as a factor in social problems the
      Gross National Happiness framework was designed to guard against.
