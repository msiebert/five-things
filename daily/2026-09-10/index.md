  - id: "2026-09-10-1"
    question: "What Japanese chemist, savoring the deep flavor of his wife's kelp broth in 1908, isolated glutamic acid from the kelp and coined a Japanese word for the taste it produced, later commercializing it as monosodium glutamate?"
    answer: "Kikunae Ikeda"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      Ikeda, a chemistry professor at Tokyo Imperial University, set out to
      identify what made his wife's kombu dashi so savory beyond the four
      known basic tastes. By evaporating a large batch of the kelp broth, he
      isolated crystals of glutamic acid and recognized a taste distinct from
      sweet, sour, salty, and bitter, which he named "umami" from the
      Japanese word for delicious. Within a year he had patented a way to
      combine glutamate with sodium to make it stable and easy to sprinkle
      on food, and by 1909 it was on shelves as Ajinomoto, the world's first
      commercial umami seasoning.
  - id: "2026-09-10-2"
    question: "What Maryland pediatrician's speculative 1968 letter to the New England Journal of Medicine, describing numbness and palpitations after eating at Chinese restaurants, is now credited with launching decades of unfounded fear around a common seasoning?"
    answer: "Robert Ho Man Kwok"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      Kwok wrote in to ask fellow doctors whether anyone else experienced
      the same symptoms he did after eating at Chinese restaurants, floating
      salt, cooking wine, or MSG as possible causes. The journal ran it as a
      lighthearted letter to the editor, but it sparked a wave of follow-up
      "research" and media coverage that coined the term "Chinese restaurant
      syndrome" and stuck it specifically to Chinese cuisine, even though
      MSG occurs naturally in many other foods eaten without complaint.
      Decades of controlled studies have since found no consistent link
      between MSG and the symptoms Kwok described, but the stigma has proven
      far more durable than the science.
  - id: "2026-09-10-3"
    question: "What three-letter U.S. FDA safety designation, shared with staples like sugar and salt, has monosodium glutamate carried since the food-additive regulatory framework was created in 1958?"
    answer: "GRAS"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      The 1958 Food Additives Amendment required new food additives to clear
      FDA review before use, but carved out an exemption for substances
      already widely recognized as safe by qualified experts. MSG has
      remained classified in that "Generally Recognized As Safe" category
      ever since, a status the FDA has repeatedly reaffirmed after reviewing
      decades of safety data, placing it alongside baking soda, vinegar, and
      table salt rather than in the tightly restricted additive category the
      public often assumes it belongs to.
  - id: "2026-09-10-4"
    question: "Among common foods, which long-aged Italian cheese can pack over 2,000 milligrams of naturally occurring free glutamate per 100 grams -- more than almost any other food, MSG-seasoned or not?"
    answer: "Parmesan"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      As Parmesan ages, enzymes slowly break down its proteins, releasing
      free glutamate that was previously locked up in long protein chains.
      A wheel aged 24 months can reach roughly 1,680 milligrams of free
      glutamate per 100 grams, and one aged 48 months can climb past 2,200
      -- among the highest concentrations found in any food, far outpacing
      tomatoes or mushrooms. It's a reminder that the compound in MSG is
      identical to glutamate the body already encounters constantly in
      unprocessed food; the seasoning just delivers it in a purer,
      more concentrated form.
  - id: "2026-09-10-5"
    question: "In what year did researchers identify a dedicated taste-bud receptor that responds specifically to glutamate, giving physiological proof that umami is a genuine fifth basic taste alongside sweet, sour, salty, and bitter?"
    answer: "2000"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      For most of the 20th century, umami was treated in the West as a
      vague, subjective descriptor rather than a real taste category. That
      changed in 2000, when researchers identified a glutamate-specific
      receptor on human taste buds -- a variant of the T1R1 receptor family
      -- that responds selectively to glutamate and is enhanced by certain
      nucleotides also found in foods like meat and dried fish. The
      discovery gave umami the same physiological grounding already
      established for the other four basic tastes, more than 90 years after
      Ikeda first proposed it existed.
