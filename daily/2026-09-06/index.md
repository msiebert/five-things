  - id: "2026-09-06-1"
    question: "What conductor led the orchestra through Stravinsky's The Rite of Spring at its chaotic 1913 Paris premiere, continuing to play despite objects being hurled at the musicians?"
    answer: "Pierre Monteux"
    topic: arts
    explanation: >-
      The premiere took place on May 29, 1913, at the Théâtre des
      Champs-Élysées, where the dissonant score and Vaslav Nijinsky's
      jarring, angular choreography split the audience into shouting,
      scuffling factions almost from the opening bars. Impresario Sergei
      Diaghilev had given strict orders to finish the piece no matter what
      happened in the hall, and conductor Pierre Monteux held the orchestra
      together as objects were thrown toward the stage, with roughly 40
      people reportedly arrested by intermission. Just a year later, a
      concert-only performance of the same score, without the shocking
      choreography, was met with wild applause — suggesting the audience's
      fury had as much to do with what they saw as what they heard.
  - id: "2026-09-06-2"
    question: "What 1940 Walt Disney animated feature paid Stravinsky $6,000 to reuse The Rite of Spring's score for a segment on the origins of life and the age of dinosaurs, a reworking he only decades later condemned as an 'imbecility'?"
    answer: "Fantasia"
    topic: arts
    explanation: >-
      Disney signed a contract in January 1939 paying Stravinsky $6,000 for
      the rights to use the score, then repurposed music originally written
      to depict a pagan sacrificial ritual into a sequence showing the
      formation of the Earth and the rise and extinction of the dinosaurs.
      At the time, Stravinsky visited the studio, raised no objections, and
      even posed for a friendly photograph with Walt Disney. It wasn't
      until much later in life that he turned sharply against the
      adaptation in interviews, dismissing Disney's reinterpretation of his
      work as "an imbecility."
  - id: "2026-09-06-3"
    question: "What music-theory term describes the technique -- layering an E-flat-seventh chord over an F-flat major chord -- behind the famous stabbing chord that opens The Rite of Spring's 'Augurs of Spring'?"
    answer: "Bitonality"
    topic: arts
    explanation: >-
      The repeated, off-beat chord that drives the "Augurs of Spring"
      section is really two unrelated chords sounding at once: an E-flat
      dominant-seventh chord in the upper strings stacked over an F-flat
      major chord in the low strings and horns. Layering two distinct
      tonalities simultaneously like this is called bitonality (or
      polytonality when more than two are involved), and it was a
      startlingly modern harmonic move for 1913. The chord's dense,
      dissonant clash, accented in irregular rhythmic bursts, became one of
      the most analyzed passages in twentieth-century music and a symbol of
      the piece's break from conventional tonal harmony.
  - id: "2026-09-06-4"
    question: "What Ballets Russes impresario, who had already scored hits with Stravinsky's Firebird and Petrushka, commissioned The Rite of Spring as a third collaboration in 1911?"
    answer: "Sergei Diaghilev"
    topic: arts
    explanation: >-
      Sergei Diaghilev's Ballets Russes had built its early reputation on
      two earlier Stravinsky scores, The Firebird (1910) and Petrushka
      (1911), both crowd-pleasing successes that made the young composer
      the company's signature voice. In 1911, Diaghilev commissioned a
      third collaboration, giving Stravinsky room to pursue a far more
      radical, primitivist vision centered on an invented ancient ritual of
      sacrifice. The three ballets are still discussed together as a
      trilogy tracing Stravinsky's rapid evolution from lush, late-Romantic
      orchestration toward the astringent, rhythm-driven modernism the Rite
      would come to define.
  - id: "2026-09-06-5"
    question: "What word, used ever since to describe the pandemonium at The Rite of Spring's 1913 premiere, is absent from every contemporary newspaper account and didn't attach to the event until a 1924 revival?"
    answer: "Riot"
    topic: arts
    explanation: >-
      Newspapers reporting on the premiere the next day described a
      "scandal," "protests," and "hissing," but none of them used that
      now-famous word. It only became attached to the event around 1924,
      during a revival of the ballet, and has since hardened into the
      standard shorthand for that night, retold and exaggerated over
      decades until it became almost mythic. Scholars examining the
      surviving eyewitness accounts note that much of the vivid detail
      commonly repeated today was actually recorded years or even decades
      after the fact, making it hard to separate what really happened from
      what the story became.
