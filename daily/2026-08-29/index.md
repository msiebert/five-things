  - id: "2026-08-29-1"
    question: "What microscopic eight-legged invertebrate, nicknamed the water bear, survives being frozen, boiled, dried out, and irradiated by shutting down into a barely-alive state called a tun?"
    answer: "Tardigrade"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      Tardigrades are millimeter-scale, eight-legged invertebrates found
      nearly everywhere on Earth, from mountaintops to the deep sea, most
      commonly in the thin films of water clinging to moss and lichen.
      When conditions turn hostile — drought, extreme cold, or radiation —
      many species can expel almost all their body water, retract their
      legs, and curl into a shriveled, barrel-shaped form called a "tun."
      In this state their metabolism drops to a tiny fraction of normal,
      essentially pausing biological time; a tardigrade can stay in this
      dormant state for years and, once rehydrated, walk away largely
      unharmed. That resilience across such a broad range of assaults —
      not just one specialty like heat or cold — is what makes tardigrades
      biology's go-to example of extreme survival.
  - id: "2026-08-29-2"
    question: "What German pastor and naturalist published the first scientific description of tardigrades in 1773, after spotting the creatures wriggling through a rain-gutter sediment sample under his microscope, and nicknamed them 'little water bears'?"
    answer: "Goeze"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      Johann August Ephraim Goeze, a German pastor with a taste for
      natural history, was examining sediment under a microscope in 1773
      when he noticed tiny, plump creatures lumbering across the slide on
      four pairs of stubby legs. He dubbed them "kleiner Wasserbär" —
      little water bear — a nickname that stuck in translation long after
      later naturalists gave the group its formal Latin name. Goeze's
      account, published as an appendix to a German translation of a
      French entomology text, is considered the first printed scientific
      description of the group, even though another naturalist may have
      spotted them earlier without publishing.
  - id: "2026-08-29-3"
    question: "What does 'Tardigrada' — the scientific name Italian biologist Lazzaro Spallanzani gave tardigrades in 1776 — literally translate to, referring to their plodding gait?"
    answer: "Slow walker"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      Three years after Goeze's water bear, Italian biologist and
      Catholic priest Lazzaro Spallanzani gave the animals their formal
      genus name, Tardigrada, drawing on their slow, rolling walk. He had
      watched them clamber across a microscope slide with the unhurried
      gait that gives the group its name, and "slow walker" has stuck as
      the scientific label ever since — even though under a microscope
      some species can move quite briskly between bouts of grazing on
      moss and algae cells.
  - id: "2026-08-29-4"
    question: "What tardigrade-specific protein, when spliced into human cell cultures, roughly halved the DNA damage those cells suffered from X-ray radiation by physically shielding their genome?"
    answer: "Dsup"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      In 2016, Japanese researchers sequencing the genome of the
      tardigrade species Ramazzottius varieornatus identified a protein
      unique to the group that they named Dsup, short for "damage
      suppressor." When they spliced the gene for Dsup into ordinary
      human cells growing in a lab dish, the modified cells suffered
      roughly half as much DNA damage as unmodified cells when both were
      exposed to X-rays. Dsup appears to work by binding to DNA and
      forming a protective cloud around it, shielding the genome from the
      free radicals that radiation and drying-out both generate — one of
      the clearest molecular explanations yet for how tardigrades shrug
      off damage that would shred human DNA.
  - id: "2026-08-29-5"
    question: "What Israeli spacecraft crash-landed on the Moon in April 2019 while carrying thousands of dehydrated tardigrades sealed inside a nickel 'lunar library' disc, raising an unresolved question about whether any of them survived the impact?"
    answer: "Beresheet"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      Beresheet, built by the Israeli nonprofit SpaceIL, was attempting to
      become the first privately funded spacecraft to land on the Moon in
      April 2019 when a technical fault sent it crashing into the surface
      at speed. Tucked inside was a DVD-sized "lunar library" from the
      Arch Mission Foundation, a nickel disc etched with millions of pages
      of human knowledge and packed with thousands of dehydrated
      tardigrades in their dormant tun state. Later lab tests simulating
      the crash's speed and shock pressure suggest the impact likely
      exceeded what tardigrades can survive, but because no one has
      recovered the wreckage, whether any of them are still intact and
      waiting for water that will never come remains an open question.
