  - id: "2026-08-23-1"
    question: "What arid depression in northeastern Ethiopia, sitting more than 100 meters below sea level at the meeting point of three tectonic plates, is regularly cited as the hottest inhabited place on Earth?"
    answer: "Danakil Depression"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      The Danakil Depression sits in Ethiopia's Afar region, near the
      borders with Eritrea and Djibouti, with parts of its floor dropping
      to around 125 meters below sea level. Average temperatures hover
      near 34°C (94°F) and regularly climb past 50°C (122°F), a combination
      of heat and low elevation that has earned it a reputation as one of
      the most punishing inhabited environments on the planet. Despite the
      conditions, the Afar people have lived and worked there for
      generations, mining the region's vast salt flats. The rest of today's
      facts dig into what makes this stretch of desert so geologically
      strange.
  - id: "2026-08-23-2"
    question: "What geological term describes the point beneath this depression where the Nubian, Somali, and Arabian tectonic plates are pulling apart, in a process expected to eventually flood the rift and form a new ocean?"
    answer: "Triple junction"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      The Danakil Depression sits directly above the Afar Triple Junction,
      one of the only places on Earth where the boundary between three
      tectonic plates is exposed on dry land rather than hidden under an
      ocean. As the plates continue drifting apart, geologists expect the
      Red Sea and Gulf of Aden to eventually breach the rift and flood it,
      turning this scorching desert into seafloor and giving the Horn of
      Africa a new coastline millions of years from now.
  - id: "2026-08-23-3"
    question: "What geothermal field in the depression, with pools so acidic they sit near pH 0 and vivid yellow-green mineral crusts, draws scientists searching for microbial life that could hint at how organisms might survive on Mars?"
    answer: "Dallol"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Dallol's hydrothermal field looks almost artificial, with sulfur and
      iron oxide staining its hot springs in shades of yellow, green, and
      orange, while temperatures in some pools reach past 100°C. The
      combination of extreme heat, near-zero pH, and high salinity makes it
      one of the most hostile environments known on Earth's surface.
      Researchers have found tiny extremophile microbes surviving there
      anyway, which is why astrobiologists study the site as a rough analog
      for what life might look like on Mars or other hostile worlds.
  - id: "2026-08-23-4"
    question: "What shield volcano in the depression, whose Afar-language name translates to 'smoking mountain,' holds one of the world's few permanent lava lakes?"
    answer: "Erta Ale"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Most volcanic craters only fill with molten lava during an eruption,
      but Erta Ale has held an actively churning lava lake within its
      summit crater almost continuously since it was first documented in
      1906, making it one of only a handful of volcanoes on Earth known to
      sustain one for decades at a time. Its name comes from the Afar
      language and roughly translates to "smoking mountain," a fitting
      description for a peak that glows visibly at night.
  - id: "2026-08-23-5"
    question: "What name was given to the standardized bars of salt, cut from the depression's flats and carried out by camel caravan, that once circulated as a form of currency across highland Ethiopia?"
    answer: "Amole"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      For centuries, workers cut salt from the Danakil's dried lakebeds
      into uniform bars called amole, roughly the size and shape of a
      thick ruler, which camel caravans then hauled hundreds of kilometers
      into the Ethiopian highlands. Amole bars circulated as everyday
      currency well into the 20th century, accepted for trade and even
      tax payments alongside coined money. Traditional Afar salt miners
      still cut and export slabs of salt from the depression today, though
      now mostly for industrial and agricultural use rather than as cash.
