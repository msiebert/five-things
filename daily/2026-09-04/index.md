  - id: "2026-09-04-1"
    question: "What English fossil collector, born in 1799 to a poor cabinet-maker's family in the seaside town of Lyme Regis, spent her life digging prehistoric marine reptiles out of the crumbling cliffs of England's Jurassic Coast to sell to collectors and scientists?"
    answer: "Mary Anning"
    topic: famous-people
    explanation: >-
      Mary Anning grew up in grinding poverty on England's south coast,
      where her father supplemented his cabinet-making income by
      scavenging fossils from the local cliffs to sell to tourists. After
      his death left the family destitute, Mary and her brother Joseph
      turned fossil-hunting into their livelihood, and Mary became
      remarkably skilled at spotting and excavating specimens that had
      eluded trained geologists. Self-taught in anatomy, geology, and
      scientific illustration, she went on to make some of the most
      important paleontological discoveries of the early 19th century —
      yet as a working-class woman, she was rarely credited by the
      gentleman scientists who bought and published her finds.
  - id: "2026-09-04-2"
    question: "At just 12 years old, Mary Anning spent months excavating the first correctly identified skeleton of what extinct marine reptile from a Lyme Regis cliff in 1811-1812?"
    answer: "Ichthyosaur"
    topic: famous-people
    explanation: >-
      Mary's brother Joseph first spotted a strange skull poking out of a
      cliff face in 1811; over the following months, twelve-year-old Mary
      painstakingly uncovered the roughly 17-foot skeleton attached to it.
      The specimen was eventually recognized as an ichthyosaur, a
      dolphin-shaped marine reptile that had gone extinct tens of millions
      of years before dinosaurs disappeared. It was the first ichthyosaur
      fossil correctly identified as such, and it helped convince
      skeptical scientists that entire species — even entire kinds of
      animal — could vanish from the Earth.
  - id: "2026-09-04-3"
    question: "A 1800 lightning strike killed the nurse and two other children sheltering with 15-month-old Mary Anning under a tree — what was the name of the nurse who died, an event locals later credited with sparking the infant's unusual liveliness?"
    answer: "Elizabeth Haskings"
    topic: famous-people
    explanation: >-
      As a baby, Mary was being minded by a nurse named Elizabeth Haskings
      when a sudden storm sent them and two other children to shelter
      under a tree. Lightning struck the tree, killing Haskings and the
      other two children instantly; Mary alone survived, reportedly
      revived by being plunged into warm water. Neighbors later claimed
      that the previously sickly infant grew unusually alert and
      energetic afterward — a folk explanation, long before anyone
      understood electricity's effects on the body, for the sharp,
      determined mind she would later bring to fossil-hunting.
  - id: "2026-09-04-4"
    question: "Inspired directly by Mary Anning's fossil finds, what 1830 painting by her friend Henry De la Beche became the first-ever pictorial reconstruction of prehistoric life based on fossil evidence, its print sales used to support Anning financially?"
    answer: "Duria Antiquior"
    topic: famous-people
    explanation: >-
      Geologist Henry De la Beche had known Anning since childhood and
      relied heavily on her discoveries and expertise. In 1830, watching
      her struggle financially, he painted "Duria Antiquior — A More
      Ancient Dorset," depicting the ichthyosaurs, plesiosaurs, and
      pterosaurs Anning had unearthed locked in a chaotic prehistoric
      seascape. He sold prints of the scene and funneled the proceeds to
      Anning; the painting is now recognized as the first widely
      circulated visual reconstruction of deep time built directly from
      fossil evidence.
  - id: "2026-09-04-5"
    question: "What term did geologist William Buckland coin for the fossilized feces Mary Anning helped identify inside ichthyosaur skeletons, which the pair used to reconstruct the diets of prehistoric marine reptiles?"
    answer: "Coprolites"
    topic: famous-people
    explanation: >-
      Anning had long noticed odd, bezoar-like stones nestled in the
      abdominal region of the ichthyosaur skeletons she excavated, and
      when cracked open they sometimes contained fossilized fish scales
      and bones. Working with her, geologist William Buckland realized
      these were fossilized droppings and coined the term "coprolites" to
      describe them. The finding let scientists reconstruct what extinct
      marine reptiles actually ate, turning what looked like curious rocks
      into direct evidence of prehistoric food chains.
