    question: "What jelly-like substance in naked mole-rat skin, a form of hyaluronan over five times larger than the human version, is credited with making the species nearly immune to cancer?"
    answer: "Hyaluronan"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      In 2013, researchers at the University of Rochester discovered that
      naked mole-rat fibroblasts secrete an unusually massive,
      high-molecular-weight form of hyaluronan, a sugar polymer that's a
      normal component of connective tissue in most animals. The oversized
      molecules make mole-rat cells extremely sensitive to crowding,
      triggering an "early contact inhibition" response that halts a
      cell's growth the moment it senses too many neighbors nearby — long
      before a tumor could ever form. When scientists experimentally
      stripped this hyaluronan out of mole-rat cells, the cells became
      vulnerable to tumors for the first time. The discovery came about
      almost by accident, after lab technicians noticed a mysteriously
      gooey substance gumming up their equipment.
  - id: "2026-08-18-2"
    question: "About how many minutes can a naked mole-rat survive total oxygen deprivation by switching its brain's fuel from glucose to fructose?"
    answer: "18"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      Nearly every mammalian brain begins dying within minutes without
      oxygen, but naked mole-rats can survive roughly 18 minutes of
      complete anoxia and emerge unharmed. A 2017 study in Science found
      that during oxygen starvation, their cells switch on the GLUT5
      fructose transporter and an enzyme called ketohexokinase, letting
      them run glycolysis on fructose instead of glucose. This
      fructose-fueled pathway sidesteps a chemical bottleneck that
      normally throttles anaerobic metabolism, so the animals can keep
      making just enough energy to survive in the suffocatingly
      overcrowded, low-oxygen burrows they call home.
  - id: "2026-08-18-3"
    question: "What social structure, otherwise found almost only in insects like ants, bees, and termites, organizes naked mole-rat colonies around a single breeding queen?"
    answer: "Eusociality"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      Naked mole-rats are one of only two known eusocial mammals, the
      other being the closely related Damaraland mole-rat. A colony of up
      to around 300 animals is dominated by a single reproductive queen
      and one to three breeding males, while every other member is
      functionally sterile and specializes in a caste-like role — digging
      tunnels, foraging for tubers, or defending the nest. The queen
      suppresses her subordinates' fertility through chemical and
      behavioral dominance, and if she dies, a fierce struggle among the
      workers determines who takes her place.
  - id: "2026-08-18-4"
    question: "What sensation, caused by acid, do naked mole-rats not feel, letting them shrug off the carbon dioxide buildup in their crowded, poorly ventilated tunnels?"
    answer: "Acid pain"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      In most mammals, acid triggers pain by activating a receptor on
      sensory neurons. Naked mole-rats carry a mutation in a related
      sodium channel that flips this response, so acid actually blocks
      pain signaling instead of setting it off. That numbness is a direct
      adaptation to their environment: their crowded underground burrows
      accumulate carbon dioxide, which mixes with body moisture to form
      carbonic acid — a condition that would otherwise be constantly
      irritating. The same insensitivity extends to capsaicin, the
      compound that makes chili peppers feel "hot."
  - id: "2026-08-18-5"
    question: "About how many years, over ten times longer than a similarly sized mouse, can naked mole-rats live, making them the longest-lived rodent known?"
    answer: "30"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      While a lab mouse rarely survives past three or four years, naked
      mole-rats have documented lifespans exceeding 30 years in
      captivity, with some individual records reported as high as 37.
      Remarkably, they show almost no signs of "senescence" — no rise in
      mortality risk, no decline in fertility, and no measurable slowdown
      in bone density or metabolism as they age. This has made them a
      major focus of anti-aging research, since understanding how they
      resist the normal mammalian aging process could point toward
      interventions for humans.
