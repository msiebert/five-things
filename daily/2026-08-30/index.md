    question: "What Hokkien Chinese word for a fermented fish sauce, introduced to southern China by Vietnamese fishermen roughly 500 years ago, gave English the word 'ketchup'?"
    answer: "Ke-tsiap"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      Long before tomatoes entered the picture, ke-tsiap was a thin, dark,
      intensely savory sauce made by fermenting fish or shellfish, a
      technique that reached southern China from Vietnamese fishermen.
      Dutch and English sailors trading in the region in the 1600s and
      1700s picked up bottles of it as a shelf-stable seasoning for long
      voyages and carried the name home with them, garbling it along the
      way into "ketchup" or "catsup." The sauce itself bore almost no
      resemblance to the modern condiment — it was closer in flavor and
      texture to soy sauce or fish sauce than to anything you'd squeeze
      onto fries today.
  - id: "2026-08-30-2"
    question: "Before tomatoes took over as the standard base, what fungus was the most popular ingredient for the ketchup recipes filling 18th-century British cookbooks?"
    answer: "Mushroom"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      Once ke-tsiap reached Britain, cooks who couldn't easily get the
      original fermented fish sauce began improvising with local
      ingredients, and mushrooms became the dominant substitute for most
      of the 1700s. Mushroom ketchup was made by salting mushrooms until
      they released their dark, savory liquid, then boiling that liquid
      down with spices into a thin, umami-rich sauce that could be bottled
      and kept for months. It was a kitchen staple in British and early
      American households well into the 19th century, used the way
      Worcestershire sauce or soy sauce is used today, and other versions
      made from walnuts, oysters, and even fruit circulated alongside it.
  - id: "2026-08-30-3"
    question: "What Philadelphia scientist published the first known tomato-based ketchup recipe in 1812, a full century after fish- and mushroom-based ketchups had already spread through Europe?"
    answer: "James Mease"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      James Mease, a physician and naturalist in Philadelphia, is credited
      with writing down the earliest known recipe for tomato ketchup in
      1812, folding the still-suspect tomato — many people at the time
      believed it was poisonous — into the established ketchup tradition
      of mushrooms and fish sauce. His version, thickened and seasoned
      without vinegar or sugar the way later recipes would be, was closer
      to a spiced tomato pulp than to the smooth, sweet-tart condiment
      that eventually won out. It took several more decades, and better
      preservation methods, before tomato ketchup edged out its
      mushroom-based rival as the default.
  - id: "2026-08-30-4"
    question: "What number, combining his own lucky digit with his wife's, did H.J. Heinz pick out of thin air for his famous ketchup-bottle slogan in 1896, despite his company already making more than 60 products?"
    answer: "57"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      H.J. Heinz spotted an advertisement for "21 styles" of shoes while
      riding a New York elevated train in 1896 and decided his own company
      needed a similarly catchy number for its slogan. At the time Heinz
      already sold more than 60 different products, but he settled on 57
      anyway, combining his own lucky number with his wife's, simply
      because he liked how it sounded. The made-up figure was plastered on
      billboards, including one of New York's first electric signs, and
      "57 varieties" has stayed on Heinz ketchup labels for well over a
      century even though it was never an accurate count.
  - id: "2026-08-30-5"
    question: "What branch of physics, named from the Greek word for 'flow,' explains why ketchup sits stubbornly in the bottle until a sharp enough tap suddenly makes it gush?"
    answer: "Rheology"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      Ketchup is what rheologists call a yield-stress fluid: it behaves
      like a soft solid, holding its shape and refusing to flow, until
      enough force is applied to break down its internal structure, at
      which point it suddenly thins out and pours freely. That's why
      tipping a bottle gently does nothing, but a sharp tap or squeeze can
      send it gushing out all at once — the shock briefly overcomes the
      threshold stress holding it together. The same yield-stress behavior
      shows up in toothpaste, mayonnaise, and wet concrete, all of which
      stay put under their own weight but flow readily once pushed past
      their breaking point.
