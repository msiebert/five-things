  - id: "2026-08-19-1"
    question: "What 19th-century technique for artificially pollinating vanilla orchids — still used on nearly every vanilla flower grown today — was invented in 1841 by a 12-year-old enslaved boy on the French island of Reunion?"
    answer: "Edmond Albius"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      On Reunion (then called Bourbon Island), colonists had planted vanilla
      orchids imported from Mexico, but the vines never produced pods
      because their only natural pollinator, a Mexican Melipona bee,
      doesn't exist outside Mexico. In 1841, Edmond Albius, an enslaved boy
      working on a plantation, worked out that a thin sliver could lift the
      flower's membrane out of the way so the pollen could be pressed onto
      the stigma by hand. The technique spread across the island's
      plantations and then the world, and remains essentially the only way
      vanilla is pollinated outside Mexico today — millions of flowers,
      each open for less than a day, hand-pollinated one at a time. Albius
      was never compensated for the discovery and died in poverty.
  - id: "2026-08-19-2"
    question: "Vanilla is often called the world's second-most-expensive spice by weight, trailing only which spice, hand-plucked three threads at a time from crocus flowers?"
    answer: "Saffron"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      Because every vanilla pod begins as a flower that must be
      hand-pollinated on the single day it opens, then hand-harvested,
      cured, and dried over months, vanilla carries some of the highest
      labor costs of any spice. Its price has swung wildly, driven by
      cyclones, drought, and speculation, at times climbing past $600 per
      kilogram — more than the price of silver. Only saffron, whose threads
      are hand-plucked three per crocus flower, consistently commands a
      higher price per kilogram.
  - id: "2026-08-19-3"
    question: "About what percentage of the world's vanilla supply comes from a single island nation, whose cyclones and droughts can send global prices swinging wildly?"
    answer: "80"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      Madagascar produces roughly 80% of the world's natural vanilla,
      concentrated in a single northeastern region called the Sava. That
      concentration makes the global supply extremely fragile: a 2017
      cyclone followed by drought wiped out huge swaths of the crop and sent
      prices spiking more than tenfold within a few years. The windfall
      brought a surge of organized theft and violence around harvest
      season, since a single sack of cured beans could be worth more than a
      farmer's annual income.
  - id: "2026-08-19-4"
    question: "What tough structural byproduct of pulping wood for paper served for much of the 20th century as the main raw material for cheap synthetic vanillin?"
    answer: "Lignin"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      Less than 1% of the vanilla flavor sold worldwide today comes from
      actual vanilla beans; the rest is synthetic vanillin. For much of the
      20th century, the cheapest source wasn't petrochemicals but lignin,
      the tough structural polymer left over when wood is pulped for paper.
      Sulfite pulp mills, especially in Scandinavia and North America,
      chemically broke lignin down into vanillin as a way of using an
      otherwise-wasted byproduct. Most synthetic vanillin today is instead
      made from a petrochemical called guaiacol, but lignin-derived "wood
      vanillin" is still produced and prized by some manufacturers for a
      fuller flavor profile.
  - id: "2026-08-19-5"
    question: "What do some Madagascar farmers prick into their still-green pods with a needle, as a kind of brand-like signature to deter theft during harvest season?"
    answer: "Tattoos"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      As vanilla prices soared, so did theft from the vine, pushing many
      farmers to sleep in their fields during harvest season. One
      lower-tech defense that spread widely is pricking a pattern of tiny
      pin-pricks, often initials or a symbol unique to the farmer, into
      each pod while it's still green on the vine. The scarring heals into
      a permanent, individually identifiable mark as the bean matures, so a
      farmer or buyer can tell at a glance whether a cured bean actually
      came from that plantation, making stolen beans harder to sell.
