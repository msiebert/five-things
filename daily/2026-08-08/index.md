    question: "What microbial process, not roasting, is primarily responsible for turning bitter, astringent raw cacao beans into the precursors of chocolate flavor?"
    answer: "Fermentation"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      Freshly harvested cacao beans are astringent and bitter, with none of
      the flavor compounds we associate with chocolate. Farmers pile the
      beans, still coated in sugary pulp, into boxes or banana-leaf heaps
      for several days, where wild yeasts and bacteria ferment the pulp.
      The heat and acids produced during this fermentation seep into the
      beans, breaking down bitter compounds and generating the amino
      acids and sugars that later react during roasting to create real
      chocolate aroma. Skip or rush fermentation and no amount of
      roasting afterward can produce good chocolate flavor — the raw
      material for it simply never forms.
  - id: "2026-08-08-2"
    question: "What dough, made from a common Aztec grain, did counterfeiters pack into hollowed-out cacao shells to pass fakes off as real currency beans?"
    answer: "Amaranth"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      Cacao beans functioned as small-denomination currency across
      Mesoamerica, valuable enough that counterfeiting became a real
      problem. According to the Spanish friar Bernardino de Sahagun, who
      documented Aztec life, dishonest sellers would carefully empty out
      real cacao shells and repack them with amaranth-seed dough, wax, or
      avocado pits to match the weight and look of genuine beans. The
      fakes were good enough that experienced merchants had to weigh
      beans against known-good ones or shake a handful to listen for a
      telltale rattle rather than trust them on sight.
  - id: "2026-08-08-3"
    question: "What Dutch chemist's 1828 invention — pressing cacao butter out of ground cacao and alkalizing what remained — created modern cocoa powder and gave 'Dutch process' chocolate its name?"
    answer: "Van Houten"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      Before 1828, drinking chocolate was a greasy, gritty beverage made
      from whole ground cacao, since no one had a practical way to
      separate out its fat. Coenraad Johannes van Houten patented a
      hydraulic press that squeezed most of the cacao butter out of
      roasted beans, leaving a dry cake that could be pulverized into fine
      powder, and he treated it with alkaline salts to mellow its harsh
      flavor and darken its color. That alkalizing step is still called
      "Dutching" today, and the extracted cacao butter it produced as a
      byproduct became the key ingredient that later let chocolatiers cast
      the first solid eating chocolate bars.
  - id: "2026-08-08-4"
    question: "What bitter alkaloid in chocolate is harmless to humans but toxic to dogs, who metabolize it far more slowly than we do?"
    answer: "Theobromine"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      Cacao naturally contains theobromine, a stimulant related to
      caffeine that affects the heart and nervous system. Human livers
      break it down efficiently, so an ordinary serving of chocolate poses
      no danger to us. Dogs, however, metabolize theobromine several times
      more slowly, so it lingers in their bloodstream long enough to build
      up to toxic levels — as little as an ounce or two of dark or baking
      chocolate can be dangerous for a small dog. The same is true, to a
      lesser extent, for cats and horses, which is why theobromine, not
      sugar or fat, is the real reason chocolate is dangerous for pets.
  - id: "2026-08-08-5"
    question: "What process — controlled heating and cooling — forces cocoa butter into its stable crystal structure, giving good chocolate its snap, gloss, and melt-in-your-mouth feel?"
    answer: "Tempering"
    topic: food
    explanation: >-
      Cocoa butter can solidify into at least six different crystal
      structures, and only one of them, known as Form V, produces
      chocolate with a glossy shine, a clean snap when broken, and a
      texture that melts smoothly right around body temperature rather
      than turning greasy or streaked with dull gray blotches. Tempering
      is the deliberate process of heating melted chocolate, cooling it to
      encourage the right crystals to form, then gently rewarming it to
      melt away the unstable ones, leaving mostly Form V behind.
      Chocolatiers who skip tempering end up with chocolate that still
      tastes fine but looks dull and melts unevenly, which is why virtually
      all commercial chocolate bars are tempered before they're molded.
