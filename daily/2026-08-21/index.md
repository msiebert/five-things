  - id: "2026-08-21-1"
    question: "What Swedish warship, built as King Gustav II Adolf's flagship during the Thirty Years' War, capsized in Stockholm harbor barely a kilometer into its maiden voyage in 1628?"
    answer: "Vasa"
    topic: history
    explanation: >-
      Vasa was commissioned as the flagship of the Swedish navy, meant to
      project royal power during the Thirty Years' War. Built at a Stockholm
      shipyard between 1626 and 1628, it carried 64 bronze cannons across two
      full gun decks — an unprecedented armament for its time — and was
      covered in hundreds of carved and painted wooden sculptures glorifying
      the king and the nation. On 10 August 1628, crowds lined the shore to
      watch it set sail. It had barely cleared the harbor when a gust of wind
      knocked it onto its side and sent it straight to the bottom, drowning
      about 30 of the roughly 150 people aboard. It lay largely forgotten on
      the seafloor for more than three centuries.
  - id: "2026-08-21-2"
    question: "About how many minutes after setting sail did a gust of wind heel the ship far enough over for water to pour through its open gun ports and send it to the bottom?"
    answer: "20"
    topic: history
    explanation: >-
      The ship's design was dangerously top-heavy: a second gun deck had been
      added late in construction at the king's insistence, piling more weight
      and more cannon high above the waterline than the ballast in the hold
      could offset. A contemporary stability test — having sailors run side
      to side across the deck — had already shown the ship rocked alarmingly,
      but with the king impatient to see his warship at sea, it sailed
      anyway. About 20 minutes and roughly 1,300 meters into the voyage, a
      moderate gust caught it broadside; it heeled over so far that water
      rushed in through the lower gun ports, which had been left open for a
      ceremonial salute, and it sank within minutes in full view of the
      crowd.
  - id: "2026-08-21-3"
    question: "What Swedish engineer spent years combing naval archives and dragging a homemade grapple through Stockholm harbor before locating the wreck in 1956?"
    answer: "Anders Franzen"
    topic: history
    explanation: >-
      Anders Franzén was an amateur naval historian and engineer who became
      convinced, after studying old records, that a well-preserved warship
      wreck could still be found in Stockholm's harbor. For years he
      systematically trawled the harbor bed with a homemade coring tool that
      could pull up wood samples without disturbing a wreck below. In August
      1956, his corer came up with a blackened piece of oak from the harbor
      floor, confirming he had found Vasa, still largely intact under about
      32 meters of water. His discovery set off the five-year campaign that
      culminated in the ship's raising in 1961.
  - id: "2026-08-21-4"
    question: "What wood-boring mollusk, common in full-strength seawater but unable to tolerate the Baltic's low salt content, is the main reason the hull survived on the seabed for over three centuries?"
    answer: "Shipworm"
    topic: history
    explanation: >-
      Shipworms are clam-like mollusks that bore into submerged wood and can
      reduce a wooden hull to rubble within a few years in ordinary ocean
      water. The Baltic Sea, however, is fed by so much river runoff that its
      salinity is far too low for shipworms to survive, which is why
      Scandinavian wrecks that would have vanished elsewhere can remain
      remarkably whole. Combined with the harbor's cold temperature and
      oxygen-poor mud, which slowed microbial decay, this let roughly 98
      percent of Vasa's original timber survive intact, an extraordinarily
      high figure for a wooden ship of its age.
  - id: "2026-08-21-5"
    question: "What corrosive substance is now slowly forming inside the raised ship's timbers, as sulfur compounds absorbed from centuries in the anoxic mud oxidize with the help of iron leaching from its corroding bolts?"
    answer: "Sulfuric acid"
    topic: history
    explanation: >-
      Long after the celebrated 1961 raising, conservators noticed pale
      yellow and white crusts forming on the hull. Analysis showed that
      sulfur compounds, absorbed into the wood from the oxygen-starved harbor
      mud over 333 years, were reacting with oxygen once the timbers dried
      out in open air. Iron from thousands of corroding original bolts and
      cannonballs acts as a catalyst for that reaction, and the sulfuric acid
      it produces attacks the wood's cellulose, threatening decades of
      preservation work. The Vasa Museum now keeps the ship in a tightly
      controlled climate and funds ongoing research into removing the sulfur
      and iron before the damage becomes irreversible.
