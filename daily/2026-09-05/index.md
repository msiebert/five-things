  - id: "2026-09-05-1"
    question: "What term describes the fiber-optic lines laid along ocean floors that physically carry the overwhelming majority of the world's intercontinental internet and phone traffic, rather than satellites?"
    answer: "Submarine cables"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      More than 500 submarine cables, spanning well over a million
      kilometers of ocean floor, form the physical backbone of the global
      internet — carrying an estimated 99%+ of intercontinental data and
      voice traffic, with satellites handling only a small sliver of the
      total. The concept dates back to 19th-century telegraph cables, but
      today's versions are hair-thin bundles of glass fiber, wrapped in
      layers of steel and polymer armor, laid and repaired by specialized
      cable ships. Despite how central satellites feel to modern
      communication, it's still this largely invisible undersea network
      that keeps continents actually talking to each other.
  - id: "2026-09-05-2"
    question: "What predator bit through an unarmored AT&T deep-sea fiber-optic cable at least four times in the mid-1980s, forcing engineers to start wrapping future undersea lines in protective armor?"
    answer: "Sharks"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      AT&T's SL undersea lightwave system, deployed starting in 1985, used
      a lightweight, unarmored 21-millimeter cable design. In waters as
      deep as 1,900 meters, sharks repeatedly bit into the cable, causing
      several outright failures — likely drawn to the faint electromagnetic
      field generated by the cable's power current rather than seeing it as
      food. The repeated damage pushed the industry to standardize thicker
      armored casings for cables in shark-prone zones, a design choice that
      persists in undersea cable engineering today.
  - id: "2026-09-05-3"
    question: "About what percentage of yearly undersea cable faults are caused by fishing gear and dragged ship anchors combined, according to industry damage records, dwarfing the share caused by sharks or sabotage?"
    answer: "80%"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      Despite the popular image of sharks gnawing through the internet's
      backbone, the leading real-world threat to submarine cables is far
      more mundane: trawler nets snagging cables on the seabed and ship
      anchors dragging across them, together responsible for around 80% of
      the roughly 200 cable faults recorded worldwide each year. Sabotage
      and shark bites make up only a small fraction of incidents by
      comparison. Repairs typically require a specialized cable ship to
      locate the break, haul the cable to the surface, splice in a new
      section, and re-lay it — a process that can take days to weeks
      depending on weather and location.
  - id: "2026-09-05-4"
    question: "What repurposed steamship, the largest vessel afloat at the time, finally laid a durable transatlantic telegraph cable in 1866 after an 1858 attempt failed within weeks?"
    answer: "Great Eastern"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      The first transatlantic telegraph cable, completed in August 1858,
      briefly let Queen Victoria and President Buchanan exchange messages —
      but its insulation was flawed and the line went dead within about a
      month. In 1866, the Great Eastern, a massive steamship originally
      built for passenger and cargo service and by far the largest ship
      afloat, carried a vastly improved cable design across the Atlantic in
      a single unbroken run, finally establishing a reliable link between
      Europe and North America. The ship later returned to recover and
      complete the failed 1865 cable attempt as well, giving the Atlantic
      two working telegraph lines within a year.
  - id: "2026-09-05-5"
    question: "What natural conductor do many submarine cable systems use to complete the electrical circuit that powers signal-boosting repeaters along their length, instead of running a second wire back to shore?"
    answer: "Seawater"
    topic: technology
    explanation: >-
      Modern submarine cables carry electrical power alongside their
      optical fibers to run repeaters — amplifiers spaced at intervals
      along the route that boost the light signal so it can travel
      thousands of kilometers without degrading. Rather than running a
      full return wire back to a second landing station, many systems use
      the ocean itself as the return path, with electrodes at each end
      completing the circuit through the conductive seawater. This
      single-wire approach simplifies the cable's construction, though it
      means the whole system depends on carefully managed grounding at
      both landing stations to work safely.
