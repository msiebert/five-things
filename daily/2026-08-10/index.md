  - id: "2026-08-10-1"
    question: "What earlier disaster, 17 years before Vesuvius erupted, had already devastated Pompeii and left much of the city still mid-repair when the eruption hit?"
    answer: "Earthquake"
    topic: history
    explanation: >-
      Pompeii is often pictured as a city frozen mid-afternoon, but by AD 79
      it had already been a construction site for nearly two decades. A
      severe earthquake struck the region in AD 62, toppling buildings,
      cracking the aqueduct, and damaging the forum and several temples.
      Scaffolding, stacked building materials, and half-finished renovations
      were still scattered through the city when Vesuvius erupted, meaning
      many residents who died were living in a town that had never fully
      recovered from the previous catastrophe.
  - id: "2026-08-10-2"
    question: "Though tradition dates Vesuvius's eruption to August 24, AD 79, autumn produce and warm clothing on victims have led many scholars to now place it in what later month?"
    answer: "October"
    topic: history
    explanation: >-
      The traditional date comes from a letter by Pliny the Younger, but
      the surviving manuscript copies disagree on the exact date, and
      physical evidence increasingly points elsewhere. Excavators have
      found pomegranates, walnuts, and other autumn produce among the
      preserved food stores, along with victims wearing heavier wool
      clothing unsuited to a Mediterranean August. A charcoal inscription
      discovered in 2018, apparently written not long before the eruption,
      even carries a date corresponding to mid-October, pushing many
      researchers to favor an eruption around October or November instead.
  - id: "2026-08-10-3"
    question: "What Italian archaeologist began pouring plaster into the body-shaped voids in Pompeii's hardened ash in 1863, casting victims in their final poses?"
    answer: "Giuseppe Fiorelli"
    topic: history
    explanation: >-
      Decomposing bodies buried in Pompeii's ash left perfectly shaped
      cavities behind once the soft tissue rotted away, but for over a
      century excavators simply dug through them, destroying the evidence.
      In 1863, archaeologist Giuseppe Fiorelli realized these voids could
      be filled with liquid plaster before excavation continued, and once
      it hardened and the surrounding ash was cleared away, it produced a
      precise cast of the victim's body, clothing, and even facial
      expression. The technique, still used today with clear resin for
      better preservation, turned Pompeii's casts into some of the most
      haunting physical records of a single moment in ancient history.
  - id: "2026-08-10-4"
    question: "What term describes the clenched, boxer-like pose baked into many Pompeii casts and skeletons, caused by heat-contracted muscles rather than a final struggle?"
    answer: "Pugilistic attitude"
    topic: history
    explanation: >-
      Many Pompeii casts show victims with raised forearms and clenched
      fists, a pose long romanticized as a desperate final struggle against
      death. Forensic pathologists now attribute it to the "pugilistic
      attitude," a passive, purely physical reaction in which intense heat
      shrinks and stiffens muscle tissue faster in the flexor muscles than
      the extensors, curling the limbs into a boxer-like stance after death,
      not during it. The same effect is well documented in modern fire
      fatalities, which is part of how researchers reconstructed just how
      fast and how hot the pyroclastic surges that killed Pompeii's
      remaining residents really were.
  - id: "2026-08-10-5"
    question: "What organ was found vitrified, turned to glass, inside the skull of a young victim at Pompeii's sister city Herculaneum?"
    answer: "Brain"
    topic: history
    explanation: >-
      In a building near Herculaneum's waterfront, researchers examining
      the skeleton of a young man found glassy, glossy black fragments
      inside his skull that turned out to be vitrified brain tissue — the
      first time this has ever been confirmed in an ancient body. The
      transformation requires the brain to be heated to several hundred
      degrees Celsius and then cooled again with extreme speed, conditions
      researchers now attribute to a fast-moving, super-heated ash cloud
      that dissipated almost immediately, rather than the prolonged burial
      that affected most other victims. It offers a rare, direct
      temperature reading of just how violent the eruption's opening
      moments were.
