  - id: "2026-08-05-1"
    question: "What ordinary object does the Icelandic word \"loki\" literally mean — a clue that may finally explain the origin of the trickster god's name?"
    answer: "Knot"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      For centuries, scholars declared the meaning of Loki's name essentially
      unknowable. Philologist Eldar Heide may have cracked it by noticing
      that Loki is repeatedly likened, in the old texts, to a knot on a
      thread — and in later Icelandic usage, "loki" itself means "knot" or
      "tangle." It's a fitting origin for a god whose defining trait is
      tangling the plans of gods and giants alike.
  - id: "2026-08-05-2"
    question: "Despite his central role in the myths, what did Loki apparently never receive from real worshippers in pre-Christian Scandinavia, unlike Odin or Thor?"
    answer: "Cult"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      Odin, Thor, and Freyr all left behind traces of active worship —
      shrines, votive objects, and place-names built from their names.
      Loki left none of that. No temple, altar, or town has ever turned up
      honoring him, which has led scholars to suspect he functioned purely
      as a narrative figure in the myths rather than a god anyone actually
      prayed to.
  - id: "2026-08-05-3"
    question: "What common modern title for Loki — \"god of ___\" — never actually appears in the medieval texts that record his myths?"
    answer: "Mischief"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      "God of mischief" feels like ancient scripture, but it isn't — the
      label doesn't appear anywhere in the Poetic Edda, the Prose Edda, or
      any other medieval source. It's a modern invention, largely from
      scholars and popular retellings trying to slot Loki into the
      cross-cultural "trickster god" archetype, a framing the original
      texts never used for him.
  - id: "2026-08-05-4"
    question: "What 12th-century Danish chronicler wrote an extensive account of Norse mythology yet never once mentioned Loki?"
    answer: "Saxo Grammaticus"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      Saxo Grammaticus's Gesta Danorum is one of the richest medieval
      sources on Norse legend, yet Loki is entirely absent from it. Since
      the surviving myths about Loki come almost exclusively from Icelandic
      and other northern Germanic sources, some scholars think he may have
      been a regional figure rather than a god known and told of across the
      whole Norse world.
  - id: "2026-08-05-5"
    question: "In what country was the Snaptun Stone found, a carved hearthstone from around 1000 CE thought to show Loki with his lips scarred shut?"
    answer: "Denmark"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      The Snaptun Stone, unearthed in Denmark and dated to roughly 1000 CE,
      is carved with a face whose lips are crossed by two scars. Most
      scholars read it as a depiction of Loki, referencing the myth in
      which the dwarf Brokkr sewed his mouth shut as punishment for losing
      a bet — making it one of the few physical artifacts that can be tied
      to Loki at all.
