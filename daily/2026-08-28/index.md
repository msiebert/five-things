  - id: "2026-08-28-1"
    question: "What stretching-and-tearing effect from a black hole's tidal forces is actually gentler at the event horizon of a giant supermassive black hole than at a small stellar-mass one — meaning an astronaut could, in theory, cross a supermassive black hole's horizon without being ripped apart on the spot?"
    answer: "Spaghettification"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      Tidal forces come from the difference in gravitational pull on the
      near and far side of an object, and that difference actually shrinks
      as a black hole's mass — and horizon size — grows. Falling toward a
      stellar-mass black hole just a few times the Sun's mass, a person
      would be stretched into a thread within seconds, well before
      reaching the horizon. But near the horizon of a supermassive black
      hole like the one at the center of our galaxy, tidal forces are
      gentle enough that a falling observer might notice nothing unusual
      at first — the fatal stretching only sets in deep inside, long after
      crossing the point of no return.
  - id: "2026-08-28-2"
    question: "What theoretical form of radiation causes black holes to slowly lose mass and eventually evaporate over almost inconceivably long timescales — implying that, despite their name, they aren't perfectly black after all?"
    answer: "Hawking radiation"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      Near a black hole's event horizon, quantum fluctuations constantly
      produce fleeting pairs of virtual particles. Occasionally the
      horizon splits a pair apart before they can annihilate each other —
      one falls in, the other escapes, carrying a sliver of energy away.
      Physicist Stephen Hawking calculated in 1974 that this steady
      trickle would slowly drain a black hole's mass, causing it to shrink
      and eventually vanish entirely. The effect is far too faint to have
      ever been directly observed for any real black hole, but it forces
      quantum mechanics and gravity to confront each other head-on.
  - id: "2026-08-28-3"
    question: "What long-standing puzzle in theoretical physics asks whether the details of whatever falls into a black hole are truly wiped out once the hole evaporates away — a direct clash between quantum mechanics and general relativity?"
    answer: "Information paradox"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      Quantum mechanics holds that information can never be truly
      destroyed, only scrambled. But Hawking's calculations suggested that
      the radiation a black hole emits as it evaporates is purely random
      and thermal, carrying no trace of what originally fell in — meaning
      everything about it would be lost forever once the hole disappears.
      That contradiction has puzzled physicists since the 1970s. Recent
      theoretical work involving so-called "islands" in a black hole's
      interior suggests the information may in fact leak out, subtly
      encoded in the radiation, but the puzzle isn't fully resolved.
  - id: "2026-08-28-4"
    question: "What hypothetical wall of extreme-energy radiation, proposed by physicists in 2012, would instantly incinerate anything crossing a black hole's event horizon — directly contradicting Einstein's prediction that a falling observer should feel nothing unusual there?"
    answer: "Firewall"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      General relativity's equivalence principle implies that, for a
      large enough black hole, crossing the event horizon should be
      uneventful — no local sign you've passed the point of no return. But
      a 2012 paper argued that preserving the rules of quantum information
      demands a blazing energy barrier right at the horizon that would
      destroy anything crossing it instantly. This "firewall paradox"
      directly contradicts general relativity's smooth-horizon picture,
      and — alongside the information paradox — remains one of the
      deepest unresolved tensions between gravity and quantum theory.
  - id: "2026-08-28-5"
    question: "What process lets some black holes end up far more massive than any single collapsing star could ever produce — layer upon layer, as smaller black holes combine with each other again and again across cosmic history, revealed by growing catalogs of gravitational-wave detections?"
    answer: "Hierarchical merger"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      LIGO and Virgo have detected dozens of black hole collisions since
      2015, and some of the resulting black holes have masses that land
      squarely inside a range physics says shouldn't be reachable by a
      single star collapsing directly — the so-called pair-instability
      mass gap. The leading explanation is that black holes born from
      dying stars combine with each other, and those merged remnants go on
      to combine again, building up mass across multiple generations
      rather than in one step. This layered growth may help explain how
      some supermassive black holes got so enormous so quickly after the
      Big Bang.
