  - id: "2026-08-20-1"
    question: "What narrow, sky-borne filament of concentrated water vapor carries most of the moisture reaching the world's mid-latitudes, flowing thousands of miles across an ocean before wringing out as rain or snow where it hits land?"
    answer: "Atmospheric river"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      An atmospheric river is a long, narrow corridor of concentrated water
      vapor in the lower atmosphere — typically only 250 to 400 miles wide but
      stretching for thousands of miles, extending from near the surface up to
      about two miles high. Fed by evaporation over the tropics, these ribbons
      carry more than 90 percent of the water vapor that moves out of the
      tropics toward the mid-latitudes at any given moment, despite covering
      only a small fraction of Earth's circumference. When one runs into a
      coastline or mountain range, the air is forced upward, cools, and dumps
      its moisture as heavy rain or snow. They are the workhorses of the
      water cycle: often gentle and beneficial, occasionally catastrophic.
  - id: "2026-08-20-2"
    question: "Two researchers coined the term \"atmospheric river\" in the early 1990s, choosing wording that stressed how slender these moisture corridors are relative to their length. At what university did they work?"
    answer: "MIT"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      The name was introduced around 1992 by Reginald Newell and Yong Zhu,
      atmospheric scientists at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. They
      chose "river" deliberately: satellite and reanalysis data showed the
      moisture didn't spread evenly across the sky but concentrated into thin,
      fast-moving filaments that behave much like rivers of water flowing over
      land. Before the term caught on, forecasters had described the same
      features loosely as "tropical moisture plumes." The river metaphor stuck
      because it captured both the narrowness and the sheer volume of water
      being moved.
  - id: "2026-08-20-3"
    question: "An average atmospheric river transports roughly 7 to 15 times as much water as pours from the mouth of which North American river, the largest by discharge in the United States?"
    answer: "Mississippi"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      A typical atmospheric river carries about 7 to 15 times the average
      water flow at the mouth of the Mississippi — and the strongest events
      move far more than that. Because the water is in vapor form spread through
      a deep column of air, the totals are staggering when it condenses. The
      transport isn't confined to the U.S. West Coast, either: a 2019
      atmospheric river blamed for deadly flooding in Iran was calculated to
      have moved, at its peak, more than 150 times the combined flow of the
      Tigris, Euphrates, Karun, and Karkheh rivers. Roughly 90 percent of the
      flood damage in the western United States traces back to these storms.
  - id: "2026-08-20-4"
    question: "One winter in the early 1860s, a weeks-long parade of atmospheric rivers turned California's Central Valley into an inland sea up to 300 miles long, bankrupting the young state and driving its legislature out of a flooded Sacramento; the USGS later modeled its 'ARkStorm' megaflood scenario on it. In what year did that flooding peak?"
    answer: "1862"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      The Great Flood of 1861–62 was fed by a relentless six-week succession of
      atmospheric rivers that battered the West Coast from Oregon to Southern
      California. Meltwater and rain drowned the Central Valley under a lake up
      to 300 miles long and dozens of miles wide, killing an estimated quarter
      of the state's cattle and destroying a comparable share of its taxable
      property. Sacramento flooded so badly that the legislature decamped to San
      Francisco, and the newly elected governor, Leland Stanford, reportedly
      rowed a boat to his own inauguration. The U.S. Geological Survey's
      "ARkStorm" scenario — a modeled modern repeat — projects damages of
      $725 billion to $1 trillion, more than a major urban earthquake.
  - id: "2026-08-20-5"
    question: "The intensity scale for atmospheric rivers unveiled in 2019 borrows the hurricane scale's 1-to-5 categories but adds a variable hurricanes ignore: a strong storm is bumped up a category when it stalls over one area past 48 hours. What is that added variable?"
    answer: "Duration"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      In 2019, Marty Ralph of Scripps' Center for Western Weather and Water
      Extremes and colleagues published a five-step scale running from Category
      1 ("weak," mostly beneficial) to Category 5 ("exceptional," mostly
      hazardous). Its two ingredients are the amount of water vapor being moved
      — a quantity called integrated vapor transport — and how long the storm
      lingers. A river that lasts less than 24 hours over a spot is knocked down
      a category, while one that persists beyond 48 hours is promoted, on the
      logic that a storm parked in place does far more damage than one that
      sweeps through. Category 5 events tend to come along only every three to
      five years.
