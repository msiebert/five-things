  - id: "2026-09-02-1"
    question: "What antibacterial enzyme did Alexander Fleming discover seven years before penicillin, after a drop of his own nasal mucus happened to dissolve bacteria on a petri dish?"
    answer: "Lysozyme"
    topic: science
    explanation: >-
      In 1921, seven years before his famous moldy petri dish, Fleming
      noticed that a drop of his own mucus had cleared away a patch of
      bacteria growing in a culture. He traced the effect to an enzyme he
      named lysozyme, present in tears, saliva, and mucus, which works by
      breaking down the cell walls of certain bacteria. Fleming himself
      later called lysozyme his best piece of scientific work, even though
      it never became a major medical treatment the way penicillin did,
      because it wasn't potent enough against most dangerous pathogens.
      The discovery did, however, sharpen his eye for exactly the kind of
      accidental antibacterial effect he'd spot again in 1928.
  - id: "2026-09-02-2"
    question: "What fruit, found moldy on a Peoria, Illinois grocery shelf in 1943, yielded the highly productive Penicillium strain that became the ancestor of nearly all industrial penicillin made since?"
    answer: "Cantaloupe"
    topic: science
    explanation: >-
      With Allied forces desperate for penicillin, US government scientists
      at a Peoria, Illinois lab spent 1943 combing local markets and soil
      samples worldwide for a mold strain that would grow faster and yield
      more than Fleming's original. A lab assistant, later nicknamed "Moldy
      Mary" Hunt, brought in a cantaloupe covered in a golden mold that
      turned out to produce roughly 200 units of penicillin per milliliter,
      far beyond anything tested before. X-ray-induced mutant strains bred
      from that same cantaloupe mold went on to seed most of the world's
      industrial penicillin fermentation tanks, a lineage that in some
      form persists in production strains today.
  - id: "2026-09-02-3"
    question: "During WWII penicillin shortages, hospitals recovered and reused the drug from what bodily fluid, since most of an injected dose passes through the kidneys chemically unchanged?"
    answer: "Urine"
    topic: science
    explanation: >-
      Early penicillin production was so laborious, requiring thousands of
      liters of mold culture to treat a single patient, that wartime
      doctors couldn't afford to let any of it go to waste. Because a large
      share of an injected dose is excreted by the kidneys still fully
      active rather than broken down by the body, hospital staff collected
      patients' urine, purified the penicillin out of it, and reinjected it
      into other patients. The practice sounds crude today, but it stretched
      desperately scarce supplies far enough to treat wounded soldiers
      until factory production caught up with wartime demand.
  - id: "2026-09-02-4"
    question: "Pfizer's WWII breakthrough in mass-producing penicillin relied on deep-tank fermentation technology the company had first developed years earlier to manufacture what common food and drink additive from mold?"
    answer: "Citric acid"
    topic: science
    explanation: >-
      Long before penicillin, Pfizer had built its business fermenting the
      mold Aspergillus niger in large vessels to produce citric acid, used
      in everything from soft drinks to candy. When the US government
      called on drug makers to scale up penicillin, Pfizer engineer Jasper
      Kane adapted that same deep-tank, submerged fermentation know-how to
      grow Penicillium mold by the thousands of gallons instead of in
      shallow trays one bottle at a time. That leap in scale, more than any
      single scientific insight, is what turned penicillin from a scarce
      laboratory curiosity into a drug that could be shipped to battlefields
      by the millions of doses.
  - id: "2026-09-02-5"
    question: "Widespread penicillin use in WWII helped cut the death rate from bacterial pneumonia among American soldiers from about 18% in the previous world war to under what percentage?"
    answer: "1%"
    topic: science
    explanation: >-
      Bacterial pneumonia and infected wounds killed enormous numbers of
      soldiers in the First World War, when doctors had no reliable way to
      stop a bacterial infection once it took hold. By the time penicillin
      reached the front lines in large quantities during World War II, it
      could clear the same infections in days, driving pneumonia deaths
      among treated soldiers down from roughly 18% to less than 1%. That
      collapse in mortality is a large part of why penicillin is remembered
      as one of the most consequential medical discoveries of the 20th
      century, not just a clever laboratory finding.
