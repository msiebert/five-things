    question: "What Nile fish, according to Egyptian myth, ate the one part of Osiris's body Isis could never recover — and lent its name to a major Egyptian city where it was later venerated?"
    answer: "Oxyrhynchus"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      In the myth, after Set dismembered Osiris's body, Isis and her sister
      Nepthys retrieved all the scattered pieces except his phallus, which
      had been eaten by a Nile fish. Ancient Egyptians identified the
      culprit as the oxyrhynchus, a species of elephantfish, and the city
      where this fish was especially venerated came to be called
      Oxyrhynchus by the Greeks. Isis compensated by magically fashioning a
      replacement, which she used to conceive her son Horus. The tale gave
      the fish a strange dual status — sacred in the city named for it, yet
      avoided as food in regions loyal to Osiris.
  - id: "2026-08-27-2"
    question: "Despite ruling over the dead, what color was Osiris's skin most often painted in Egyptian art — a hue tied to fertile Nile silt and sprouting crops rather than to decay?"
    answer: "Green"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      Osiris is regularly depicted with green skin (sometimes black),
      symbolizing the dark silt the Nile's floods deposited on the fields
      and the green shoots of new crops. Far from marking him purely as a
      god of death, this made him primarily a god of renewal and
      agricultural fertility, with his death and rebirth mirroring the
      annual cycle of planting and harvest. Some temple reliefs even show
      grain sprouting directly from his mummified body, reinforcing the
      link between his myth and the harvest.
  - id: "2026-08-27-3"
    question: "What ancient pillar-shaped symbol, said to represent Osiris's restored backbone, was ceremonially \"raised\" during royal jubilee festivals to affirm a pharaoh's stability and continued rule?"
    answer: "Djed"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      The djed pillar, a stylized column with several horizontal crossbars
      near its top, became closely associated with Osiris's spine after
      Isis and Nepthys reassembled his body. Pharaohs performed a "raising
      the djed" ceremony, often during the Sed festival marking a king's
      jubilee, symbolically re-erecting Osiris's backbone and, by
      extension, the stability of the kingdom itself. Some scholars believe
      the symbol predates Osiris worship entirely, originally representing
      a stripped tree trunk tied to agricultural fertility before being
      folded into his myth.
  - id: "2026-08-27-4"
    question: "At what Upper Egyptian city, believed to hold Osiris's actual burial site, did pilgrims gather annually for one of history's earliest recorded dramatic reenactments — a passion play depicting his death and resurrection?"
    answer: "Abydos"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      Abydos was Egypt's principal cult center for Osiris, home to a
      cenotaph long believed to be his tomb. Each year crowds attended the
      "Mysteries of Osiris," a multi-day festival in which priests and
      townspeople acted out his murder by Set, Isis's search for his body,
      and his resurrection — a structured dramatic performance historians
      consider among the oldest religious theater on record, predating
      Greek drama by well over a thousand years. Surviving inscriptions
      from officials who directed the rites, such as Ikhernofret under
      Senusret III, describe the roles and processions in detail.
  - id: "2026-08-27-5"
    question: "How many pieces did Set cut Osiris's body into, according to one widely cited version of the myth — a number some scholars link to Egypt's traditional provincial divisions, each of which claimed to hold a piece?"
    answer: "42"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      One version of the myth, recorded by the Greek writer Plutarch, has
      Set scattering Osiris's dismembered body across Egypt in as many
      pieces as there were nomes — Egypt's traditional administrative
      provinces, numbering 42. Each nome could then claim a local relic or
      burial site of the god, giving every region a personal stake in his
      cult and knitting the country's local religious traditions into one
      unified national mythology around a single divine king. Earlier and
      regional versions of the myth cite other numbers, such as fourteen
      pieces, showing how the story was adapted for different symbolic
      purposes.
