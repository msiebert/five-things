  - id: "2026-08-17-1"
    question: "What kind of ultra-dense stellar remnant, left behind when a massive star's core collapses in a supernova, packs more mass than the Sun into a sphere about the size of a city?"
    answer: "Neutron star"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      A neutron star is what's left when a star roughly 8 to 25 times the
      Sun's mass runs out of fuel and its core collapses under its own
      gravity. The implosion is so violent that it crushes protons and
      electrons together into neutrons, halting only when neutron
      degeneracy pressure pushes back — leaving a sphere only about 20
      kilometers across, roughly the size of a city, yet holding more mass
      than the entire Sun. The outer layers of the dying star are blown off
      as a supernova, while this crushed core survives at the center as one
      of the densest objects in the universe.
  - id: "2026-08-17-2"
    question: "About how many billion tons would a sugar-cube-sized piece of neutron-star material weigh if you could bring it to Earth?"
    answer: "1"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      Neutron star material is so tightly packed — comparable to the
      density inside an atomic nucleus — that a piece no bigger than a
      sugar cube would weigh about a billion tons on Earth, roughly the
      mass of a mountain. This density is why neutron stars have such
      immense surface gravity, typically around 200 billion times Earth's;
      an object dropped from just a meter up would hit the surface at a
      meaningful fraction of the speed of light. Only a black hole packs
      matter more densely without erasing it entirely.
  - id: "2026-08-17-3"
    question: "What pulsar, spinning about 716 times per second, is the fastest-rotating neutron star known?"
    answer: "PSR J1748-2446ad"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      Discovered in 2004 in the globular cluster Terzan 5 using the Green
      Bank Telescope, PSR J1748-2446ad completes a full rotation in about
      1.4 milliseconds — roughly 43,000 revolutions per minute. At that
      spin rate, a point on its equator is moving at close to a quarter of
      the speed of light. Such "millisecond pulsars" reach these speeds by
      siphoning matter and angular momentum from a companion star over
      millions of years, spinning up an old, otherwise slowing neutron star
      like a top.
  - id: "2026-08-17-4"
    question: "What type of neutron star, with a magnetic field trillions of times stronger than Earth's, unleashed a December 2004 flare so powerful it measurably disturbed Earth's ionosphere from 50,000 light-years away?"
    answer: "Magnetar"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      On December 27, 2004, the magnetar SGR 1806-20, on the far side of
      the Milky Way, released more energy in about a fifth of a second than
      the Sun emits in roughly 100,000 years. The burst was so intense it
      briefly bounced radiation off the Moon, saturated satellite
      detectors, and measurably disturbed Earth's ionosphere despite
      traveling 50,000 light-years to reach us. Magnetars generate the
      strongest magnetic fields known in the universe — up to a
      quadrillion times Earth's — thought to arise from a dynamo effect
      during the neutron star's violent birth, and their giant flares are
      believed to be triggered by sudden fractures, or "starquakes," in
      their ultra-rigid crusts.
  - id: "2026-08-17-5"
    question: "What gravitational-wave event from a 2017 neutron-star merger confirmed that such collisions forge heavy elements like gold and platinum?"
    answer: "GW170817"
    topic: space
    explanation: >-
      On August 17, 2017, LIGO and Virgo detected gravitational waves from
      two colliding neutron stars, and within seconds telescopes around the
      world caught the collision's afterglow in gamma rays and visible
      light from a galaxy about 130 million light-years away. The event
      confirmed a long-standing theory: that the extreme, neutron-rich
      environment of a neutron-star merger is where "r-process"
      nucleosynthesis can forge the universe's heaviest elements. Astronomers
      estimated the collision produced roughly an Earth's mass worth of
      gold alone, offering a real answer to where precious metals like gold
      and platinum actually come from.
