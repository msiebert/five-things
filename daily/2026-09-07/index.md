  - id: "2026-09-07-1"
    question: "What major Mesoamerican deity, honored as a wind god, patron of priests, and embodiment of the planet Venus, was worshipped continuously from Teotihuacan's earliest temples through the height of the Aztec Empire?"
    answer: "Quetzalcoatl"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      Quetzalcoatl's name comes from Nahuatl words for the quetzal bird's
      tail feather and for snake, and images of a plumed serpent deity
      appear as far back as the Teotihuacan civilization of the 3rd to 8th
      centuries CE, long before the Aztecs rose to power. As his cult
      spread across later cultures, he accumulated an unusually wide
      portfolio: the Toltecs linked him to the morning and evening star,
      and the Aztecs came to credit him with inventing the calendar and
      books and with protecting priests and craftsmen. Few Mesoamerican
      deities were worshipped across so many centuries and civilizations
      without their core identity being replaced outright.
  - id: "2026-09-07-2"
    question: "What Spanish historian, who never set foot in the Americas, first published in 1552 -- three decades after the conquest -- the now largely discredited claim that the Aztecs mistook Hernan Cortes for a returning god?"
    answer: "Lopez de Gomara"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      The popular story that Moctezuma II welcomed Hernan Cortes in 1519
      because he believed the conquistador was Quetzalcoatl fulfilling a
      prophecy of return does not appear in any account from the conquest
      itself. It first shows up in print in 1552, in a history written by
      Francisco Lopez de Gomara, who compiled his account in Spain from
      secondhand testimony rather than firsthand experience. Many modern
      historians view the tale as a later invention that cast the Spanish
      conquest as a peaceful, almost divinely ordained handover rather than
      a violent military campaign, and argue Moctezuma's actual response to
      Cortes was better explained by shrewd political caution.
  - id: "2026-09-07-3"
    question: "What unusual shape did Aztec temples dedicated to Ehecatl, Quetzalcoatl's wind-god aspect, typically take, so that gusts could pass around the building without a flat wall to catch them?"
    answer: "Round"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      Most Mesoamerican temples were rectangular stepped pyramids, but
      shrines built for Ehecatl -- the wind-god manifestation of
      Quetzalcoatl, usually shown wearing a distinctive beaked mask --
      were built round instead. Archaeologists have uncovered several of
      these circular platforms, including one beneath the historic center
      of modern Mexico City, and the design is widely interpreted as a
      deliberate accommodation to the god's domain: a curved structure
      lets wind flow around it rather than slamming into a flat facade the
      way it would with a conventional temple.
  - id: "2026-09-07-4"
    question: "In Toltec legend, the exiled priest-king Topiltzin-Quetzalcoatl vowed to return in a specific calendar year that, by an eerie coincidence later exploited by Spanish chroniclers, also matched the year Hernan Cortes landed in Mexico. What was that calendar year called?"
    answer: "Ce Acatl"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      According to Toltec legend, the priest-king Ce Acatl Topiltzin
      Quetzalcoatl was shamed into leaving his city of Tula after a rival
      god tricked him into drunkenness and disgrace, and he traveled east
      to the coast, where some versions say he burned himself on a pyre
      and rose as the morning star, while others say he sailed away on a
      raft of serpents, vowing to return in the year Ce Acatl, or "1 Reed."
      Under the Aztec calendar's 52-year cycle, 1519 -- the year Cortes
      arrived -- happened to fall on a 1 Reed year, a coincidence colonial-era
      writers later leaned on heavily to link the conquest to the old
      prophecy.
  - id: "2026-09-07-5"
    question: "In one Aztec creation myth, Quetzalcoatl traveled to the underworld Mictlan and gathered the bones of a previous, destroyed humanity, then used what substance of his own to bring a new human race to life?"
    answer: "Blood"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      Aztec cosmology held that the world had already been created and
      destroyed four times before the current age, each time wiping out
      the humanity living in it. To repopulate the fifth world, Quetzalcoatl
      is said to have descended into Mictlan, the underworld, and retrieved
      the bones of the people from the previous ages, sometimes after
      tricking or outrunning the lord of the dead. He ground the bones into
      meal and pierced his own body to let his blood fall over them,
      an act of self-sacrifice that animated the first humans of the
      present era.
