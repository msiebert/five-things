  - id: "2026-08-12-1"
    question: "What geospatial engineer calculated the exact coordinates of Point Nemo in 1992 using computer software, without ever sailing there himself?"
    answer: "Hrvoje Lukatela"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Point Nemo, the spot in the South Pacific farthest from any land, was
      never "discovered" by an explorer — it was computed. In 1992, Croatian
      survey engineer Hrvoje Lukatela used geospatial software to find the
      point on Earth's surface that maximizes distance from the three
      nearest coastlines, arriving at 48°52.6'S 123°23.6'W. He named it after
      Captain Nemo, the submariner from Jules Verne's "Twenty Thousand
      Leagues Under the Sea," whose name in Latin literally means "no one."
  - id: "2026-08-12-2"
    question: "Because Point Nemo sits so far from any coastline, the crew of what orbiting outpost, passing about 400 km overhead, are often the closest humans to it?"
    answer: "ISS"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Point Nemo lies roughly 2,700 km from the nearest land, and even that
      land is uninhabited rock. That puts it so far from human settlement
      that when the International Space Station passes overhead in low
      Earth orbit, at an altitude of about 400 km, its crew is frequently
      physically nearer to Point Nemo than any person on the surface of the
      planet — a reminder of just how much of the ocean is still emptier
      than space feels.
  - id: "2026-08-12-3"
    question: "What 120-ton Russian space station was deliberately deorbited and sunk into the Point Nemo spacecraft graveyard in 2001?"
    answer: "Mir"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Point Nemo's extreme remoteness and sparse shipping traffic make it
      the preferred dumping ground for retired spacecraft, since any surviving
      debris is unlikely to hit anything. In 2001, Russia's Mir space
      station, which had orbited Earth for 15 years and hosted dozens of
      crews, was deliberately deorbited and broke apart on reentry, with its
      surviving pieces sinking into the waters near Point Nemo. It remains
      one of the largest objects ever deliberately sunk there.
  - id: "2026-08-12-4"
    question: "What massive rotating ocean current starves the waters around Point Nemo of nutrients, making it one of the most biologically lifeless patches of sea on Earth?"
    answer: "South Pacific Gyre"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Point Nemo's isolation isn't just about distance from land — its
      waters are also unusually empty of life. The region sits inside the
      South Pacific Gyre, a huge, slow rotating current system that traps
      the area away from nutrient-rich currents and coastal runoff. With
      little upwelling to feed phytoplankton, the surrounding ocean has some
      of the lowest biological productivity of any marine region on Earth,
      making it an ideal spot for sunken debris to go largely undisturbed.
  - id: "2026-08-12-5"
    question: "About how many decommissioned spacecraft have nations deliberately sunk at Point Nemo since 1971?"
    answer: "300"
    topic: geography
    explanation: >-
      Since the Soviet Union first used the area in 1971, space agencies
      from the United States, Russia, Japan, and Europe have deorbited
      nearly 300 spacecraft into the waters around Point Nemo, earning it
      the nickname the "spacecraft cemetery." Retired cargo craft, defunct
      satellites, and Mir are all resting there, and the International Space
      Station itself is slated for the same fate when it is finally
      decommissioned.
