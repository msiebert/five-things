    question: "What antimatter particle do the most powerful lightning-linked gamma-ray bursts, called terrestrial gamma-ray flashes, fire out into space?"
    answer: "Positrons"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      Thunderstorms don't just make light and thunder — some produce
      terrestrial gamma-ray flashes (TGFs), millisecond-long bursts of
      gamma radiation with energies up to 20 million electronvolts, roughly
      500 of which occur worldwide every day. Inside a TGF, relativistic
      electrons collide with air molecules and generate gamma rays
      energetic enough to knock electron-positron pairs directly out of the
      atmosphere. NASA's Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope has caught these
      positron beams arriving from thunderstorms below, meaning ordinary
      storms on Earth are, in a very real sense, manufacturing antimatter
      and launching it toward orbit.
  - id: "2026-08-09-2"
    question: "What high-energy particles raining down from deep space are now thought to help seed the runaway electron avalanches that trigger lightning inside storm clouds?"
    answer: "Cosmic rays"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      How lightning actually gets started inside a thundercloud has long
      been one of atmospheric physics' biggest unsolved puzzles, since the
      electric fields storms build up seem too weak on their own to spark a
      bolt. One leading explanation is that cosmic rays — high-energy
      particles constantly arriving from outside the solar system — strike
      the upper atmosphere and kick loose electrons that get accelerated by
      a storm's electric field. Those electrons collide with more air
      molecules, freeing still more electrons in a runaway avalanche, and
      researchers increasingly think this cosmic-ray-seeded cascade is what
      tips a charged cloud over into a full lightning strike.
  - id: "2026-08-09-3"
    question: "What term describes the reddish, jellyfish-shaped electrical flashes that shoot from thunderstorm tops up into the mesosphere, first caught on video by accident in 1989?"
    answer: "Sprites"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      Above an active thunderstorm, a whole separate category of electrical
      activity flickers far too high and too faint to notice from the
      ground. Sprites are reddish-orange discharges, often shaped like
      jellyfish or carrots, that reach 50 to 90 kilometers up into the
      mesosphere in response to a powerful lightning stroke below. They
      went undocumented until July 1989, when a University of Minnesota
      researcher testing a low-light camera for a rocket experiment left it
      running overnight and accidentally captured two frames of one on
      tape — despite scattered anecdotal sightings going back to the 1730s,
      that accidental video was the first hard proof they were real.
  - id: "2026-08-09-4"
    question: "What nickname do volcanologists use for the lightning storms that erupt inside a volcano's own ash plume?"
    answer: "Dirty thunderstorm"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      Erupting volcanoes can generate their own lightning storms, nicknamed
      "dirty thunderstorms" because the electrical charge builds up in a
      plume of ash rather than water droplets. As magma shatters into
      particles of wildly different sizes, they collide at high speed and
      strip electrons from one another through triboelectric charging, and
      rising ice-coated ash higher in the plume adds a second charging
      mechanism much like an ordinary storm's. The oppositely charged
      regions separate as the plume rises, and when the imbalance grows
      large enough, lightning tears through the ash cloud — sometimes
      producing thousands of flashes an hour, visible for miles around an
      eruption.
  - id: "2026-08-09-5"
    question: "What glassy, root-shaped tube of fused silica does a lightning strike leave behind when it hits sand?"
    answer: "Fulgurite"
    topic: nature
    explanation: >-
      A direct lightning strike can carry a channel briefly hotter than
      2,000 degrees Celsius, easily enough to melt the silica in dry sand
      instantaneously. As the current burns downward and branches through
      the ground, it fuses sand grains into a hollow, glassy tube that
      traces the exact path the bolt took, then cools and hardens within
      seconds as the surrounding sand insulates it. These formations are
      called fulgurites, from the Latin word for thunderbolt, and diggers
      have unearthed branching, root-like specimens over two feet long —
      essentially a fossilized, three-dimensional record of one single
      lightning strike frozen in glass.
