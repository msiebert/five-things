  - id: "2026-08-24-1"
    question: "What Hungarian physician, working in a Vienna maternity ward in the 1840s, discovered that a simple hand-hygiene practice could slash deaths from childbed fever decades before germ theory was accepted?"
    answer: "Ignaz Semmelweis"
    topic: famous-people
    explanation: >-
      Ignaz Semmelweis was an obstetrician at Vienna General Hospital in
      the 1840s, where he noticed something troubling: mothers giving
      birth in the ward staffed by doctors and medical students died of
      childbed fever at far higher rates than those in the ward staffed by
      midwives. He eventually traced the difference to doctors arriving at
      the maternity ward straight from autopsies, without washing their
      hands, and ordered a hand-hygiene routine that cut deaths
      dramatically. His work predates the acceptance of germ theory by
      decades, and he died without seeing it vindicated.
  - id: "2026-08-24-2"
    question: "What colleague's death from a scalpel wound picked up during an autopsy gave Semmelweis the key insight linking invisible 'cadaverous particles' to the deaths of new mothers on his ward?"
    answer: "Jakob Kolletschka"
    topic: famous-people
    explanation: >-
      Jakob Kolletschka, a colleague of Semmelweis, died after accidentally
      cutting himself with a scalpel while supervising a student autopsy.
      Semmelweis was struck by how closely Kolletschka's rapid decline and
      symptoms resembled those of the women dying of childbed fever on his
      ward, and concluded that both had been poisoned by the same
      invisible "cadaverous particles" carried on unwashed hands and
      instruments from the autopsy room. It was the insight that led
      directly to his handwashing protocol.
  - id: "2026-08-24-3"
    question: "What chemical solution did Semmelweis order doctors to scrub their hands in before examining maternity patients, a change that cut his ward's death rate from over 18% to around 1%?"
    answer: "Chlorinated lime"
    topic: famous-people
    explanation: >-
      Starting in May 1847, Semmelweis required everyone entering his
      maternity ward to first wash their hands in a bowl of chlorinated
      lime solution, chosen for its strong smell as a way to neutralize
      any lingering "cadaverous particles" from the dissection room. The
      results were dramatic and nearly immediate: mortality from childbed
      fever in his division fell from roughly 18% to about 1% within the
      year, one of the starkest natural experiments in the history of
      medicine.
  - id: "2026-08-24-4"
    question: "What term, now used across science and medicine generally, describes the reflexive rejection of new evidence that contradicts accepted belief — the very reaction Semmelweis's hand-hygiene findings met from his own colleagues?"
    answer: "Semmelweis reflex"
    topic: famous-people
    explanation: >-
      Despite his results, the broader medical establishment largely
      rejected his findings, unwilling to accept that doctors themselves
      were spreading disease. Colleagues mocked him, and his own hospital
      contract wasn't renewed. Decades later, historians and scientists
      coined a term for this exact pattern of reflexively dismissing
      well-supported new evidence because it conflicts with prevailing
      belief — now applied well beyond medicine, to any field where
      established experts resist an inconvenient discovery.
  - id: "2026-08-24-5"
    question: "What blood infection, the same broad category of illness Semmelweis had devoted his career to preventing, ultimately killed him two weeks after he was committed to an asylum and allegedly beaten by guards?"
    answer: "Sepsis"
    topic: famous-people
    explanation: >-
      Worn down by rejection, Semmelweis grew increasingly erratic and was
      committed to a Viennese asylum in 1865, reportedly restrained and
      beaten by guards there. He died just two weeks later, at 47, from a
      wound infection that spread through his bloodstream. The final
      irony was inescapable: the man who had discovered how to prevent
      fatal bloodstream infections in new mothers died of one himself,
      years before the medical world accepted he had been right all
      along.
