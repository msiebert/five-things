    question: "What group of roughly 500 brilliantly colored marine crustacean species, not true shrimp at all but a separate lineage called stomatopods that split off over 400 million years ago, is famous for having some of the fastest and most powerful strikes in the animal kingdom?"
    answer: "Mantis shrimp"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      Mantis shrimp are stomatopods, a lineage of predatory crustaceans that
      diverged from the ancestors of crabs and true shrimp over 400 million
      years ago and now numbers roughly 500 known species. They get their
      name from the way their folded, spring-loaded front limbs resemble a
      praying mantis's raptorial arms, and different species use those arms
      either to smash armored prey like crabs and snails or to spear
      soft-bodied prey like fish. Most live in burrows on tropical and
      subtropical reefs and are prized by divers for their vivid, often
      iridescent coloring. The rest of today's facts dig into the biology
      behind their reputation as one of the ocean's most formidable small
      predators.
  - id: "2026-09-09-2"
    question: "When a mantis shrimp's club-like claw strikes prey, the collapsing cavitation bubble it leaves behind briefly flashes to a temperature comparable to the surface of what astronomical object?"
    answer: "The Sun"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      A smashing mantis shrimp's dactyl club accelerates through water so
      violently -- on the order of 10,000 times the acceleration of gravity,
      about as fast as a .22 caliber bullet leaving a rifle -- that it
      briefly carves a near-vacuum cavity behind it. When that cavity
      collapses, it produces a phenomenon called sonoluminescence: a flash
      of light and a burst of heat that spectral measurements put at over
      20,000 Kelvin, hotter than the Sun's surface, for a fraction of a
      millisecond. That means unlucky prey effectively gets hit twice --
      once by the club itself, and once by the shockwave and heat from the
      imploding bubble.
  - id: "2026-09-09-3"
    question: "How many distinct types of color-sensing photoreceptors do mantis shrimp eyes contain -- four times what humans have, yet oddly not put to use for finer color discrimination?"
    answer: "12"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      Humans see color using just three types of photoreceptors, so mantis
      shrimp eyes, packing up to 12 different types plus separate receptors
      for ultraviolet and polarized light, seem like they should have
      vastly superior color vision. A 2014 study testing their ability to
      tell similar hues apart found the opposite: mantis shrimp performed
      far worse than expected, unable to distinguish colors that even a
      honeybee with far fewer receptor types can separate easily.
      Researchers now think mantis shrimp identify colors by rapidly
      scanning their many channels across an object, more like a barcode
      reader tagging an identity than a human eye comparing shades.
  - id: "2026-09-09-4"
    question: "What term, borrowed from a French crystallographer who first described the pattern in mollusk shells, names the twisted, helicoidal stacking of mineral fiber layers inside a mantis shrimp's claw that helps filter out damaging shear waves from its own strikes?"
    answer: "Bouligand structure"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      A smasher's dactyl club survives thousands of strikes it inflicts on
      others thanks to a layered internal architecture. Beneath a hard
      mineral impact surface, bundles of mineralized fibers are arranged in
      a herringbone pattern, and deeper still those bundles are stacked so
      each layer is rotated slightly from the one beneath it -- a
      helicoidal arrangement known as a Bouligand structure, named for the
      crystallographer Yves Bouligand. This twisting stack acts like a
      phononic shield, scattering and filtering out the high-frequency
      shear waves from an impact before they can crack the club. Materials
      scientists are studying the same structure to design tougher body
      armor and impact-resistant aerospace materials.
  - id: "2026-09-09-5"
    question: "What common name is given to a spearing species of mantis shrimp whose mated pairs can stay bonded to the same partner and share a single burrow for up to 20 years?"
    answer: "Zebra mantis shrimp"
    topic: animals
    explanation: >-
      Unlike the smashing species better known for their violent strikes,
      the zebra mantis shrimp (Lysiosquillina maculata) hunts by spearing
      soft-bodied prey with barbed, blade-like appendages. It's also one of
      the few known monogamous mantis shrimp species: mated pairs share a
      single burrow, cooperate in guarding and caring for their eggs, and
      can remain bonded to the same partner for up to 20 years -- an
      unusually long-term pairing for an invertebrate that can itself live
      around 40 years.
