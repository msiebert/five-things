  - id: "2026-08-16-1"
    question: "What trickster spider-god from Akan folklore in Ghana is credited as the source of all stories, giving Ghanaian folktales their collective name?"
    answer: "Anansi"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      Anansi (also spelled Ananse or Kwaku Ananse) is a half-man, half-spider
      trickster from the oral traditions of the Akan people of Ghana,
      especially the Ashanti. In many tales he is the son of the sky god
      Nyame and acts as a go-between linking gods and humans, repeatedly
      outwitting stronger, more powerful figures through wit rather than
      force. Over centuries his stories crossed the Atlantic with the
      transatlantic slave trade and took root across the Caribbean,
      Suriname, and the American South, where he survives today under names
      like Anancy and Aunt Nancy.
  - id: "2026-08-16-2"
    question: "According to Akan legend, which sky god sold Anansi the rights to all the world's stories, in exchange for him capturing a python, a leopard, a swarm of hornets, and a menacing forest spirit?"
    answer: "Nyame"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      In the foundational Anansi myth, all stories originally belonged to
      the sky god Nyame, who set an outrageous price for handing them over.
      Anansi pulls off the impossible tasks through cunning rather than
      strength — tricking the python into testing itself against a stick,
      luring the hornets into a gourd with a fake rainstorm, and using a
      sticky gum doll to snare the mischievous forest spirit. Impressed,
      Nyame hands over the stories, which become known as "Anansi's
      stories" — an origin tale explaining why storytelling itself is
      considered Anansi's domain.
  - id: "2026-08-16-3"
    question: "What Akan word, literally meaning something like \"spider tales,\" became the general term Ghanaians use for any folktale, whether or not Anansi appears in it?"
    answer: "Anansesem"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      Because Anansi is credited with originating storytelling itself, the
      Akan folded his name directly into their word for stories in general:
      anansesem. A storyteller opening a tale still often begins with a
      ritual call-and-response, asking listeners' permission to tell an
      "anansesem," even when the story that follows has nothing to do with
      the spider trickster himself. The word is a small piece of linguistic
      evidence for how central Anansi became to the very idea of narrative
      in Akan culture.
  - id: "2026-08-16-4"
    question: "In one Anansi tale, to what part of his own body did he foolishly strap a pot containing all the world's wisdom before trying to climb a tree with it?"
    answer: "Stomach"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      Wanting to hoard all wisdom for himself, Anansi supposedly gathered it
      into a single pot and tied it to his stomach so he could stash it atop
      a tall tree, out of everyone else's reach. Climbing proved impossible
      with the pot in front of him, and when his young son pointed out he'd
      have climbed easily with it strapped to his back instead, an
      embarrassed and furious Anansi hurled the pot to the ground. It
      shattered, scattering wisdom across the world in pieces — the tale's
      explanation for why no single person holds all knowledge, and why
      wisdom turns up in unexpected people.
  - id: "2026-08-16-5"
    question: "What name did enslaved Africans' descendants in the American South give to Anansi as his stories took root in Gullah and Southern oral tradition?"
    answer: "Aunt Nancy"
    topic: mythology
    explanation: >-
      Anansi's stories crossed the Atlantic during the transatlantic slave
      trade and adapted to new settings, surviving as "Anancy" in Jamaica
      and "Kompa Nanzi" in the Netherlands Antilles. In the American South,
      particularly among Gullah communities, the trickster was reimagined as
      "Aunt Nancy," a folk figure whose stories of outwitting the powerful
      carried the same undertone of resistance and hope that scholars trace
      in the related Br'er Rabbit tales. The persistence of these renamed
      variants across the diaspora reflects how enslaved communities
      preserved and reshaped their oral heritage under difficult conditions.
